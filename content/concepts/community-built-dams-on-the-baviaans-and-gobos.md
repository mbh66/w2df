## The goal

> The valley holds more of its own rain in its mountains and soils, with small dams that residents build and look after themselves.

## Why the valley needs it

**Two rivers from the mountains.** The Baviaans comes down the kloof above Genadendal, and the Gobos comes out of the mountains northeast of Greyton [MS]. Both run into the Riviersonderend below Theewaterskloof Dam [IC].

**Water the valley already depends on.** In 1899 the Moravian Church bought the mountain farm Langgezocht, about 1,881 hectares, to protect Genadendal's water sources [MS]. Since 1854 Greyton's leiwater furrows have carried water from the Gobos to the plots that hold a water right [MS]. See [[history/leiwater|Greyton's Leiwater]].

**Water that must stay in the rivers.** In August 2022 the Minister set the Reserve for the Breede and its tributaries, including the Riviersonderend, the Gobos, and the Baviaans [IC]. The Reserve is the water a river must keep for people's basic needs and for its own health, and it comes before other uses [IC]. See [[rules/national-water-act|National Water Act]].

**Children are already looking for sites.** In the [[cultural/education/project/index|Class 6 Project]], each child uses Google Earth to find a gully in the valley where they would build a leaky dam [MS].

## The idea

The idea is to find out which dams the valley could build itself, and where. A map study (see *How the sites were found*) points to three sizes.

### 1. Leaky dams in side gullies

A leaky dam is a low wall of stones, logs, or packed branches across a gully. It slows rainwater so that it soaks into the soil and lets the rest pass. It holds no lake. Volunteers and clearing teams could build leaky dams with hand tools, starting in small side gullies away from the main rivers.

### 2. Low walls of 5 metres or less in the kloofs

The map study found seven places in the mountain kloofs where a short wall closes off a narrow valley. A wall of 5 metres or less at these sites would hold roughly 10,000 to 200,000 cubic metres [MS]. An Olympic swimming pool holds about 2,500 cubic metres, so this is between 4 and 80 pools.

A wall of this height stays below the size the law treats as a dam with a safety risk (see *What the law asks*). A community group could build it with the help of an engineer.

### 3. Higher walls are a different kind of project

The same sites could hold far more water behind higher walls: up to about 9 million cubic metres behind a 40 metre wall above Greyton [MS]. Every wall above 5 metres that the study tested would also hold more than 50,000 cubic metres [MS]. Such a dam must be registered as a dam with a safety risk [IC]. That puts it outside what a community could build on its own. The figures are listed below so the Forum can see what the valley's terrain allows.

## How the sites were found

The study used the free elevation map from NASA's Shuttle Radar Topography Mission, which gives one height for about every 30 metres of ground [MS]. A computer traced each river down its valley and tested walls of 5 to 60 metres at points along it [MS]. At each point it measured the length of the wall and the water that would lie behind it [MS]. The sites listed are the ones where a short wall holds the most water [MS].

The study has limits:

- **Heights are rough.** A 30 metre map smooths out narrow kloofs. Real walls may be shorter and riverbeds deeper, so the figures could be out by a third either way [MS]. For 5 metre walls the map is at the edge of what it can show.
- **The valley floor is too flat.** Below the kloofs the map cannot show small rises and falls, so the study gives no sites there [MS].
- **It shows the shape of the land only.** It says nothing about rock, soil, plants, animals, who owns the land, or whether a dam would fill.

Before any site is chosen, someone would need to walk it and check the ground and rock where the wall would stand.

## What the law asks

- **Dam safety.** A dam that can hold more than 50,000 cubic metres and has a wall more than 5 metres high is a dam with a safety risk under the National Water Act [IC]. Its owner must register it [IC].
- **Storing water.** The national general authorisation for storing water covers only storage off the river, in a dam beside it [IC]. A dam built in the river itself falls outside it [IC], so it would most likely need a water use licence [TBV].
- **Work in a river.** Whether a leaky dam in a side gully needs any permission has not been checked [TBV].
- **The Reserve.** Any dam on the Baviaans or the Gobos would have to leave the Reserve flowing in the river [IC].

The [[organisations/breede-olifants-catchment-management-agency|Breede-Olifants Catchment Management Agency]] issues water use licences in the Breede catchment [IC].

## Who would benefit

- **Growers and food gardens** near Genadendal and Greyton, if stored water could be used for them.
- **The rivers in the dry months**, if water held in the soil reaches them later in the year.
- **Firefighters**, who would have water close to the mountain slopes.
- **Homes and businesses that need electricity.** Water running down from a kloof dam could turn a small turbine and make electricity. Two lakes on the same river, one high and one low, could also work as a battery. Water would run down through the turbine when power is needed, and be pumped back up at night, when electricity is cheaper on time-of-use tariffs [TBV]. On the Baviaans, B2 sits about 240 metres above B4 [MS]. A rough calculation for 5 metre walls at both sites gives about 30,000 kilowatt-hours, after losses, each time the lower lake fills and the water is sent back down [MS]. If a home uses about 10 kilowatt-hours a day, that is enough for about 100 homes for a month. On the Gobos, G1 sits only about 60 metres above G3, so the same amount of water would give about a quarter as much electricity [MS]. Whether any of this would pay, and how it would connect to the grid, has not been looked at.
- **Volunteers and clearing teams**, who could learn to build and look after small water works.
- **Learners**, who could study real sites in their own valley.

## Who would pay

Nothing has been costed [MS]. Leaky dams cost little more than labour and local stone or wood. A 5 metre wall would need an engineer's design and materials, and perhaps a licence.

## Who would decide

- **On the Baviaans:** the kloof above Genadendal lies on Farm 39 land or on Langgezocht, the mountain farm the Moravian Church bought to protect Genadendal's water [TBV]. Any work there goes to the Transformasie Komitee first. See [[villages/farm-39|Farm 39]].
- **On the Gobos:** the kloof northeast of Greyton may lie inside the Greyton Nature Reserve, which Theewaterskloof Municipality manages with support from the Greyton Conservation Society [TBV]. Holders of Greyton's leiwater rights draw on the same river [MS].
- **Water use:** the Breede-Olifants Catchment Management Agency and the Department of Water and Sanitation, under the National Water Act.

## How it fits the Forum's goal

The Forum's [[long-term-goal|long-term goal]] plans for the valley to keep more of what it has, including water in the ground. Small dams are one way to hold the valley's rain.

It serves two of the Forum's [[priorities/index|priorities]]: [[priorities/conservation-environment|Conservation and Environment]], and [[priorities/agriculture-food-sovereignty|Agriculture and Food Sovereignty]].

## What is still open

- **The land.** Who holds the land at each site. The Transformasie Komitee and the Moravian Church have not been asked about the Baviaans sites. The municipality and the Greyton Conservation Society have not been asked about the Gobos sites.
- **Leiwater.** How a dam on the Gobos would affect the flow that feeds Greyton's furrows. The holders of leiwater rights have not been asked.
- **Whether the dams would fill.** How much water each kloof gives in a year. The study did not look at rainfall or river flow.
- **The law.** Which permissions each size of dam would need. The catchment agency has not been asked.
- **The rivers' life.** What a wall would do to the plants and animals in the river and below it.
- **Mud and sand.** How fast each lake would fill with silt.
- **The ground.** Whether the rock and soil at each site can hold a wall.

## What happens next

1. The Transformasie Komitee, the Moravian Church, the municipality, the Greyton Conservation Society, and the holders of leiwater rights are asked for their views.
2. The idea is set down for a Forum meeting.
3. If the Forum wants to go further, volunteers walk the sites with someone who knows small dams, and check the figures on the ground.
4. The catchment agency is asked which permissions each size of dam would need.
5. A first leaky dam could be built in a side gully where the landholder agrees.
