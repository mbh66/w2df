The DevForum works from one ethos. We call it community economy, or *gemeenskapsekonomie* in Afrikaans. We want the Valley of Grace to be a place where people can come and see what a community economy looks like, and feel what it's like to take part in one. Everything the DevForum does should fit the ethos, from the five [[priorities/index|DevForum Priorities]] to the smallest initiative on [[register|The Register]].

## A place to come and see it

People seldom change what they believe because of an argument. More often, something happens to them first. The writer Charles Eisenstein makes this point in *Deep Tara*. A new belief, he writes, tends to follow a change in the person, often after an experience that showed them something they hadn't seen before [MS]. He writes of joining up "islands of coherence" where a new story is being lived [MS].

The valley could be one of those places. Think of someone who comes for a weekend. On Saturday morning they walk a stretch of river with the team that cleared it of alien trees. At lunch they eat food from valley farmers who own the shop together. By Sunday they've put in a morning's work and been fed for it, and they've seen how a pool of promises works from the inside. None of this exists yet in that form. Each piece is a proposal on this site, and [[priorities/how-ethos-shapes-priority|How the Ethos Shapes Each Priority]] says where.

Some of it has started. Since July 2026, residents, learners and visitors have been able to record plants, animals and heritage on the Valley of Grace Nature Realm, which covers about 10,000 hectares of the valley [MS].

Visitors have come to look at this valley before. Governor Janssens came in 1806 and gave the mission the name Genadendal [IC]. In 1813 a surveyor mapped the mission's boundary, and the residents were given no proof that they owned their land [MS]. President Mandela came on 10 October 1995 [IC]. The dates and their sources are on [[history/history-of-the-valley|History of the Valley]]. So the first question about any visit is who owns what the visitor comes to see.

Tourism was the first priority the DevForum agreed at its first meeting [MS]. Under this ethos, a visitor takes part in the valley's work, and the money and the story stay with the people who made what the visitor came for. Nobody is on display. A household, a congregation or a farm opens its door to visitors only if it chooses to, and on its own terms.

## What we mean by community economy

Picture the valley's economy as an iceberg:

![[Gibson-Graham-Iceberg.png]]
A diverse economy, as depicted by J.K. Gibson-Graham

Above the water is the part that typically gets counted: wages, shop sales, rent, the price of a bag of potatoes. Below the water is the bigger part. An ouma looks after the neighbour's children. Someone passes seedlings over a fence. A congregation cooks for a funeral. In Greyton, gardens take their turn at the [[history/leiwater|leiwater]], as they have since 1854 [MS].

The geographers Katherine Gibson and Julie Graham, who wrote together as J.K. Gibson-Graham, drew the economy this way in 2006 [TBV]. They called the whole of it, above and below the waterline, a diverse economy. A community economy is the part where people decide together how they live with each other and with the land [TBV].

This site reads the valley's history through the [[history/the-time-model|TIME model]], which has four forms of organisation: Tribes, Institutions, Markets and Emergent. A community economy belongs to the newest, Emergent, in which people pool what they hold towards things none of them could do alone. It isn't yet the main form anywhere in the valley. Its first signs date from 2011, and they're listed on [[history/the-valley-through-time|The Valley Through TIME]]. An emergent community economy, in our words, is one that's still forming. Visitors would see it as it forms.

For the DevForum, it means the valley keeps more of what it has and decides together how to share it. A rand spent here passes through more valley hands before it leaves the valley. Work, skills, water, and soil stay here too.

## Why we use this word

Some of the ideas behind this page come from essays the writer Charles Eisenstein published in 2026 [MS]. He writes of a "parallel society" that grows beside the existing one and trades with it [MS].

In South Africa, "parallel" carries a hidden meaning, and so does *parallelle samelewing*. It can call up apartheid's separate development, and the families forced out of Greyton to [[villages/heuwelkroon|Heuwelkroon]] under the Group Areas Act [IC]. The DevForum's work is about joining the valley up. So we use community economy, a term from research that doesn't carry the same history.

We left one of Eisenstein's ideas out. His brief describes money and attention being steered quietly, out of view [MS]. The DevForum makes its decisions at its meetings, and [[civic/governance/between-meetings/index|Between Meetings]] records each meeting it holds with outside partners in between [MS].

## How it fits with the BioConomy and Vorming

[[long-term-goal|Our Long-Term Goal]] names two things beneath the DevForum's work. The [BioConomy](https://bioconomy.earth) is how we plan to get there: co-operatives, shared agreements, and pools people pay into and draw from. [[concepts/vorming|Vorming]], still a proposal, is how people grow in responsibility for the valley through shared work on its land and water.

Community economy holds both. The BioConomy is its toolkit. If the DevForum adopts Vorming, it becomes the ethos's inner side, because tools only work when the people using them are ready to carry the responsibility of what they build.

Vorming also says why visitors matter to the DevForum. It proposes that residents who guide visitors along a river they restored take on more responsibility for that river, and that fees from visitors and courses would help pay bursaries for the valley's own young people.

## The questions we ask of every initiative

Before an idea is tabled, the person bringing it answers these questions in writing, and the answers go to the meeting with it. Initiatives already on the Register are asked the same questions once a year. Any answer can be talked through at the meeting, and a "no" blocks nothing on its own. Instead, it opens up further discussion.

### 1. Does more of what the valley has stay in the valley?

Money, work, skills, water, soil. At its first meeting, the DevForum set out to build local supply chains so that more of the money spent in the ward stays in the ward [MS]. Ask where a rand goes after it's spent here, and how many valley hands it passes through.

### 2. Were the people it affects there from the start?

The DevForum agreed at its [[civic/governance/minutes/2026-09-16-inaugural-meeting|first meeting]] that affected communities should be involved from the start of a proposal [MS]. Ask who was in the room when the idea took shape, and who still hasn't been asked.

Where land or water is held in trust, or its ownership is still being settled, the body with the mandate is asked first. For Farm 39 land, that is the [[civic/governance/genadendal-transformasie-komitee|Genadendal Transformasie Komitee]] [MS].

### 3. Does it trade with the economy that's already here?

A community economy buys from the businesses already in the valley and sells to them. It fills the gaps they leave, and it grows beside them. Ask whether the initiative could stand if one funder walked away.

### 4. If it's young, is it protected while it grows, and does it know when it will stand in the open?

New ways of doing things seldom survive full competition on their first day. The researchers Adrian Smith and Rob Raven found that protection works when it ends in what they call empowerment, where the new practice is made ready to stand on its own [IC]. They wrote that empowerment was the least studied part of the field [IC]. They warn that protection can be captured by the people who gain from it, and can go on sheltering work that isn't improving [IC].

A new cooperative can work things out among its members before it shows the wider valley. Ask what is being protected, from what, and on what date it opens up. [[concepts/protecting-young-initiatives|Protecting Young Initiatives in the Open]] suggests a way to record the answers.

### 5. Does it build on what people already share?

Elinor Ostrom studied fisheries, forests and irrigation systems that people had shared for generations. She found that the ones that lasted were run by the people who used them, under rules they made and watched over themselves [TBV].

Ask whether the initiative strengthens what people already share, or crowds it out. Money can do the crowding. A review of 128 experiments found that rewards can weaken people's own reasons for doing a task [IC].

### 6. Does it help the people who take part grow in responsibility for the valley?

This is the question Vorming would ask of every initiative, as set out on [[long-term-goal|Our Long-Term Goal]].

### 7. Does every village gain?

Our Long-Term Goal says: "A good result in one village doesn't count if another village is left behind" [MS]. Ask which villages gain, which pay, and whether the gap between them grows. Ask, too, whether the initiative divides people, or takes what isn't ours to take.

### 8. Could someone from outside the valley come and take part?

Not every initiative needs visitors. A young one may need time out of view first, as question 4 asks. Where an initiative is ready, ask what a visitor would do there with their own hands. Ask who meets them and who earns from the visit. Ask, too, whether the people whose heritage or home the visitor sees agreed to it, and on what terms.

### A caution about counting

When a number becomes a target, people start working to the number [TBV]. So the DevForum counts what it must, says why, and asks the people being counted what the numbers miss. The [[political-economy-of-the-valley|Political Economy of the Valley]] will meet this question first, in conversations across the valley planned for January and February 2027 [MS].

## What is still open

1. When protection for a young initiative should end, and who decides.
2. Whether a stretch pool or a voucher can run outside the laws on financial services and payments. This needs legal advice.
3. Whether buyers in the Overberg will accept a label growers give each other.
4. Whether stokvels, or something like them, fit the valley.
5. How the Political Economy Analysis can listen to people without changing what they say.
6. How many visitors each village wants, and who decides that.
