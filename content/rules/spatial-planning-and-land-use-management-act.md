The Spatial Planning and Land Use Management Act, known as SPLUMA, is the national law that sets the framework for planning across South Africa [IC]. Parliament passed it in 2013, and it came into force on 1 July 2015 [IC]. It makes the municipality the first decision-maker on how land may be used, requires every municipality to have a spatial development framework and a single land use scheme for its whole area, and sets principles that every planning decision must follow [IC]. In the Western Cape it works together with the [[rules/western-cape-land-use-planning-act|Western Cape Land Use Planning Act]] and the municipality's own [[rules/municipal-land-use-planning-by-law|Municipal Land Use Planning By-law]].

## What it does

- **Five principles.** Every planning decision must follow the development principles in section 7: spatial justice, spatial sustainability, efficiency, spatial resilience, and good administration [IC]. Spatial justice includes redressing past spatial and other development imbalances, and improving access to and use of land by disadvantaged communities [IC].
- **Spatial development frameworks.** National government, each province, and each municipality must have one [IC].
- **One land use scheme for the whole municipality.** Each municipality must adopt a single land use scheme for its entire area (section 24) [IC]. The scheme must include provisions that permit land use management and regulation to be introduced step by step in rural areas, areas under traditional leadership, informal settlements, and areas not previously covered by a scheme [IC].
- **The scheme is law.** A land use scheme has the force of law, binds everyone including the state, and land may only be used for the purposes the scheme allows (section 26) [IC].
- **The municipality decides.** Land use applications are decided by the municipality, through a Municipal Planning Tribunal or an official it authorises, with an internal appeal [IC].

## What it means in the valley

**In Greyton and Heuwelkroon.** Land has carried zoning under a scheme for a long time, and the Act mainly changes who decides and by which principles. A resident who wants to run a guest house or a shop from home, or to subdivide a plot, applies to the municipality under its [[rules/municipal-land-use-planning-by-law|Municipal Land Use Planning By-law]].

**On Farm 39.** The mission settlements were governed for most of the twentieth century by laws for "coloured rural areas", and their land was not covered by an ordinary town planning scheme [TBV]. The Act's requirement that one scheme cover the whole municipal area, and its provision for introducing land use rules step by step in rural areas and areas not previously under a scheme, speak directly to land like [[villages/farm-39|Farm 39]]. How far Theewaterskloof's scheme has in fact been applied to the land in Genadendal, Bereaville, Voorstekraal, Boschmanskloof, and Madiba Park is a question for the municipality's planners, and this site has not yet had an answer [TBV]. Because the Minister still holds most of the land in trust, a land use application there also needs the consent of the owner, which may be the hardest step.

**Spatial justice.** The principle of redressing past spatial imbalances is the one most often cited where two communities with different land histories sit side by side, as Greyton and Genadendal do. See [[villages/next-door-different-rules|Next Door, Different Rules]].
