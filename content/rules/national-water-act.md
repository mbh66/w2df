The National Water Act is the law that governs all water in South Africa [IC]. It made the national government the public trustee of the country's water, ended private ownership of water, and set aside a Reserve for basic human needs and for the health of rivers [IC]. It came into force in 1998 [IC]. In the valley it governs who may take water from the Riviersonderend, the Gobos, the Baviaans, and the furrows that feed Greyton's gardens, and it is the law under which any idea to restore the valley's water would have to work.

## What it does

- **Water is held in trust.** The national government is the public trustee of the nation's water resources, and must ensure that water is used for the benefit of all people (section 3) [IC].
- **The Reserve comes first.** For each water resource a Reserve is set: the water needed for basic human needs, and for the ecological health of the river. It has priority over other uses [IC].
- **Using water needs a basis in law.** Taking water, storing it, or diverting a river is a water use under section 21 [IC]. A person may use water only on one of these bases [IC]:
  - **Schedule 1 use**, without any authorisation: water for reasonable domestic use, small gardening that is not for commercial purposes, and watering animals (other than feedlots), taken from a source on or next to one's land;
  - **existing lawful use**: a use that was lawfully exercised in the two years before the Act came into force, until it is replaced by a licence (section 32);
  - **a general authorisation**, published for a kind of use or an area (section 39);
  - **a licence** issued by the Department or the catchment management agency (section 40).
- **Users can organise.** The Act provides for water user associations, which let users co-operate on a shared resource (Chapter 8) [IC], and for catchment management agencies that manage water in a whole catchment (Chapter 7) [IC].

## What it means in the valley

**The Reserve for the valley's rivers.** In August 2022 the Minister set the Reserve for the Breede and its tributaries, including the Riviersonderend, the Gobos, and the Baviaans [IC]. Any new use in the valley must leave that water in the rivers. See [[concepts/from-water-access-to-water-agency|From Water Access to Water Agency]] for the figures.

**Greyton's leiwater.** Plots in Greyton have drawn water from the Gobos through open furrows since 1854 [MS]. Those rights predate the Act, and are understood to continue under it, most likely as existing lawful use [TBV]. How they are recorded today, and whether they have been verified by the Department, is not yet known [TBV]. See [[history/leiwater|Greyton's Leiwater]].

**Farm 39.** The mission bought the mountain farm Langgezocht in 1899 to protect the water sources of Genadendal [MS]. Water rights that go with Farm 39 land are part of what is held in trust, and their use and restoration go to the Transformasie Komitee for review under this site's rules. Which rights exist, and in whose name they are registered, is not recorded here [TBV].

**Farmers on the Riviersonderend.** Farmers along the river are entitled to water released from Theewaterskloof Dam [IC]. The [[economic/farmers-land-water/zonderend-water-users-association|Zonderend Water Users Association]] brings together the people who use the river's water [MS].

**Clearing invasive plants.** Invasive trees use water that would otherwise reach the rivers [IC]. Clearing them does not itself need a water use licence [TBV], but any plan to count or sell the water it returns, as the water agency idea proposes, would have to work through this Act and the Department.

**Households.** Collecting rainwater and taking water for household use and a small food garden from a source on one's own land is Schedule 1 use and needs no authorisation [IC]. A food garden that sells its produce is commercial, and is not covered by Schedule 1 [TBV].
