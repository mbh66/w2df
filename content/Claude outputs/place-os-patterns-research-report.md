# Place OS: The Pattern Across Four Places

Research report for the Ward 2 Development Forum (W2DF). Research run of 4 November 2026. Websites were read on 3 or 4 November 2026 unless a date is given.

**How to read the tags.** Each factual claim is followed by one of three tags.

- **[IC] Independently Corroborated.** Confirmed by an official record (a law, a court judgment, a parliamentary reply, an Auditor-General report, a government statement) or by at least two sources that do not depend on each other.
- **[MS] Mission-Sourced.** From the account of the party the claim is about, its partners or funders, the W2DF's own records, or Michael Haupt's own documents. This includes the Forum's own reading of its own history.
- **[TBV] To Be Verified.** Plausible and unconfirmed.

Claims carried from the three case-study pages on the W2DF register keep the tag the page gives them. Sentences marked *Inference* are the researcher's own reading. Michael Haupt's hypothesis, his framing of the trend and his definition of Place OS are [MS] throughout: this report tests them and does not treat them as established.

**A note on sources.** Almost everything known about the three cases comes from the initiatives themselves, their partners or reporters who quote them. The independent record consists mainly of court judgments, parliamentary reports, Auditor-General reports and provincial statements, and these say more about the state than about the initiatives. Outcome evidence is therefore thin for every case, and the report keeps self-reports apart from independent findings. Several Valley of Grace concept pages cited here say they are not yet agreed and ask readers not to share them; this report refers to them only as proposals, in the words the prompt already uses. The report was written on the day of the local government elections of 4 November 2026. It gives each party's view only where a public record holds it, in that party's words.

## 1. Findings in brief

**The pattern.** Across the four cases, three things recur with moderate evidence. A small, named core carries each initiative [MS in every case]. Each builds a device that holds people together: a school and trust in Bonnievale, a forum, Compact and levy in Mafube, eight project categories in the Waterberg, and a ward forum with five priorities in the Valley of Grace [MS; Mafube's Compact IC]. And each meets the state at the point of signature, where Bonnievale's deal with the province went ahead [MS] and Mafube's Compact was never signed [IC]. Michael Haupt's five-step hypothesis [MS] holds only in part. Coherence (step 2) is supported in every case. Attraction (step 3) is supported in Bonnievale and weak elsewhere. Scanning (step 1) is not shown by any of the three outside cases: each began from a deficit, a crisis or a mine closure, and none published an asset scan [MS; Mafube's crisis IC]. Transfer (step 4) is clear in one case, Bonnievale, where a community trust co-funds a public school with a willing province [MS; collaboration school status IC]. In Mafube the transfer of electricity to a private company in 2011 came eight years before the business forum [IC], so the order runs the other way. In the Waterberg no function moved. Origin (step 5) cannot be tested: none of the three case pages records its place's deep story. **Main exception:** the Valley of Grace itself, which has a documented scan and a recorded deep story before any transfer. The hypothesis fits the valley better than the cases it was drawn from (*Inference*).

**The trend.** The evidence supports a narrower claim than the one tested. In South Africa, citizens in rich and poor areas increasingly supply their own services [IC: Anciano et al., 2026], and the official record shows why: 62 municipalities in a severe financial position and 174 without enough cash to pay creditors in 2024/25 [IC], 63% in financial distress by the Minister of Finance's count [IC], and R122.5 billion in overdue municipal debt to Eskom at 31 August 2026 [IC]. But most of what residents and business do falls into four separate routes: litigation to make the state perform, co-provision through a statute, private concessions, and voluntary self-help. Only co-provision has a law behind it. Internationally, steady movement of functions to places appears where a statute and a budget travel together (Kerala, Indonesia, Scotland, LEADER) [IC], and the same record shows 924 services returning to municipal ownership between 2000 and 2019 [IC: TNI]. National Treasury's own 2026 response moves water and electricity towards utilities and implementing agents [IC]. **Strength:** strong for the fact of state distress; moderate for the existence of a trend towards place-level provision; weak for any claim that the trend runs one way. **Main exception:** remunicipalisation where the state is capable, and the 2026 national policy shift.

**The name.** No established name covers all five steps. Co-production, external service delivery and community asset transfer cover transfer; ABCD covers the scan; collective impact and community-led local development cover coherence and attraction [IC for each source]. Only Regenesis's Story of Place and Marshall Ganz's public narrative put a story at the centre of the method [IC], and a 2026 Story of Place article already uses the words "operating system" for the long-term part of the work [MS]. *Inference:* Place OS would add the pairing of an operating system with a contested, plural origin story and the question of state functions, which none of these covers together. It would duplicate Story of Place on the story and collective impact on the shared layer. **Main exception:** the name itself is in use. PlaceOS is an Australian smart-building platform [IC], OS Places is an Ordnance Survey product [IC], and Land OS and Town OS are existing software names [MS]. Trademark registers were not searched.

**The differences that matter most.** Only one of the three outside cases moved a state function into the hands of a community body, and it did so in partnership with a province that kept ownership and running costs (Bonnievale) [MS]. Mafube's electricity transfer went to a private company and was eight years older than the business forum that now defends it [IC]. WDI facilitates and lobbies; no function moved [MS]. The three cases also differ in which sphere of government holds the function: school education is a national and provincial matter, electricity reticulation a municipal one, and tourism is shared between spheres [IC: Constitution, Schedules 4 and 5]. *Inference:* the sphere that holds the function, and whether it is willing to sign, decided more about the form of each initiative than anything the founding group did. **Order of the steps.** The cases contradict the order in two places. In Bonnievale and Mafube a deficit or a crisis came first, and no formal scan was found at any point [MS; Mafube's crisis IC]. In Mafube the transfer came first and the coherence device came eight years later [IC]. *Inference:* the hypothesis may describe the Valley of Grace better than it describes the cases it was drawn from. A more general statement that fits all four would be: a trigger (crisis, deficit, closure or invitation) gathers a small group; the group builds a coherence device; the device draws some outside actors; and transfer of a function happens only where a sphere of government that holds the function agrees to it, or has already contracted it out.

**Counter-patterns.** Three findings count against the hypothesis as stated. First, the clearest "transfer" among the cases (Mafube's electricity) is a private concession whose legality the national recovery plan proposes to reassess [IC], and whose benefits the Mayor, SALGA, SAMWU, the EFF and the Namahadi Business Forum disputed before Parliament [IC]. ## 4. The trend in South Africa

### 4.1 The state's capacity to deliver local services, from official records

**Audit outcomes.** The Auditor-General's consolidated report on local government for 2024/25, tabled on 24 June 2026, recorded 39 clean audits (15%), 117 unqualified opinions with findings, and 99 modified opinions (qualified, adverse or disclaimed), of which 8 were disclaimers [IC]. Compared with 2020/21, 72 municipalities improved, 145 stayed the same and 38 regressed [IC]. Disclaimed opinions fell from 35 in 2019/20 to 8 in 2024/25 [IC]. The share of unqualified opinions reached 61%, a level last seen in 2015/16 [IC]. Irregular expenditure was R40.14 billion in 2024/25 and R145.21 billion since 2021/22 [IC]. A clean audit means the accounts are reliable and the auditors found no material problems with performance reporting or compliance with law.

**Financial health.** The same report found 62 municipalities (24%) in a severe financial position, 54 that disclosed uncertainty about whether they could continue operating, 174 (72%) without enough cash to pay their creditors, and 116 (45%) that adopted unfunded budgets [IC]. Water distribution losses were R14.73 billion and electricity losses R21.63 billion in 2024/25 [IC]. Of 35 wastewater treatment works inspected, 22 discharged effluent that did not meet standards [IC]. In February 2026 the Minister of Finance told Parliament that 63% of municipalities were in financial distress, and the Deputy Minister put the number at 162 [IC]. Treasury used section 216(2) of the Constitution to stop national transfers to 75 municipalities in persistent breach of the MFMA [IC]. In October 2026 the Minister of Finance said four municipalities were under national intervention under section 139(7) with mandatory recovery plans: Lekwa, Enoch Mgijima, Mangaung and Ditsobotla [IC].

**Section 139 interventions.** Section 139 of the Constitution lets a province, and in some cases national government, step in when a municipality fails to meet its duties. A Parliamentary Monitoring Group compilation of interventions from 2010 to March 2025 lists 168 municipalities under intervention across all nine provinces: North West 63, KwaZulu-Natal 40, Eastern Cape 18, Free State 13, Western Cape 10, Mpumalanga 9, Gauteng 6, Limpopo 5 and Northern Cape 4 [IC]. These are entries, and some municipalities appear more than once. In the Western Cape, four were ongoing in March 2025: Beaufort West, Kannaland, Garden Route District and Theewaterskloof [IC]. The Parliamentary Budget Office found that 39 municipalities were under section 139 interventions in 2020/21 to 2022/23, and concluded that the interventions "have often not produced lasting improvement" [IC]. It found that only one of the municipalities under intervention before 2020/21 exceeded national averages on service delivery, and that one was Mafube [IC].

**Debt to Eskom and the water boards.** Eskom reported municipal arrear debt of R2.6 billion at 31 March 2014 and R58.5 billion at 31 March 2023 [IC]. The Minister of Electricity and Energy gave overdue municipal debt as R120.26 billion at 31 July 2026, with 83 municipalities in Eskom's formal debt-recovery processes and 71 of them in National Treasury's Municipal Debt Relief Programme [IC]. He gave R122.5 billion at 31 August 2026, with 110 municipalities each owing more than R1 million [IC]. Municipal debt to the water boards was given as R21.3 billion by the Minister of Water and Sanitation in August 2024 [IC] and R23.7 billion in a parliamentary briefing in November 2024 [IC].

*Inference:* the official record shows two things at once. Audit compliance improved over the term that ended with these elections, and financial distress, unpaid bulk bills and failing works grew over the same years.

Only the second route has a statute behind it.

### 4.3 The legal channels

- **Municipal Systems Act 32 of 2000, sections 76 to 81.** A municipality may deliver a service through an external mechanism, including "a community based organisation or other non-governmental organisation legally competent" to do so, under a service delivery agreement [IC]. Section 78 sets the steps before it may decide [IC]. Jaap de Visser (Dullah Omar Institute, November 2025) writes that the process is "complicated, time-consuming and costly", that "the law favours internal service delivery mechanisms", and that "the municipality remains responsible for the service" [IC: academic legal analysis].
- **MFMA 56 of 2003, section 33.** A contract that binds a municipality for more than three years needs public notice, views from the national and provincial treasuries and a council resolution [IC]. Mafube's case page records that none of these steps was found for the electricity agreement [MS].
- **Intergovernmental Relations Framework Act 13 of 2005.** It sets up forums and implementation protocols between spheres of government [IC]. It does not provide for community bodies as parties.
- **District Development Model.** A national approach, launched in 2019, for all spheres to plan and budget together district by district; the Waterberg was one of three pilots [IC].
- **Small-town regeneration.** SALGA runs a Small Town Regeneration programme with yearly conferences [TBV], and a CoGTA strategy of the same name is reported from 2021 [TBV]. Neither document was read in this run.
- **National Treasury's 2026 Budget Review.** It announced a shift of water and electricity towards a "utility model" and the use of implementing agents for grant-funded infrastructure [IC]. This is the most recent national policy, and it moves functions to professional bodies, without a place-level role named.
- **Provincial school law.** The Western Cape Provincial School Education Amendment Act 4 of 2018 gives collaboration schools, with a non-profit operating partner holding at least half the governing body seats, a legal basis [IC]. The High Court (2023) and the Supreme Court of Appeal (September 2026) dismissed a challenge by Equal Education and SADTU [IC].

### 4.4 Older South African precedents

**Mission stations before 1909.** Genadendal began in 1738 as a mission school at Baviaanskloof, where residents and missionaries built water furrows, gardens, a forge, a mill and a school for themselves [IC/MS: History of the Valley]. By the 1830s it ran a school, a kindergarten, a lending library, a teachers' training college (1838) and a printing press [IC/MS]. In 1858 the land was granted to the Moravian Church in trust for its residents [MS]. *Inference:* for more than a century the mission provided, within its own boundaries, much of what a state would later provide, under the church's authority and without residents' title to land. Elim, founded by Moravians in the Overberg in 1824, followed the same pattern [TBV].

**The Mission Stations and Communal Reserves Act of 1909.** The Act placed mission land under management boards; residents kept the right to live on the land and could not own it [IC]. At Genadendal, Balie's history records that many residents left the church over the Act [MS], and a secular board took over in 1924 [IC]. *Inference:* the 1909 Act moved control of the place's services away from the place, towards boards answerable to the colonial and then Union state.

**The Rural Areas Act (House of Representatives) 9 of 1987.** This Act governed the former mission and communal areas, the "coloured rural areas", under the tricameral system; Genadendal was added to it in 1989 [IC]. It was repealed when TRANCRAA came into force [IC].

**TRANCRAA, 1998.** The Transformation of Certain Rural Areas Act 94 of 1998 came into force on 2 November 1998 [IC]. It provides for land held in trust in these areas to pass to a municipality, a Communal Property Association or another body approved by the Minister [IC]. A parliamentary reply of November 2025 lists transfers to four municipalities and 61,802 hectares to "other legal entities", and does not mention Genadendal [IC]. Genadendal's transitional period ended in January 2011 without a transfer [IC].

### 4.5 The counter-evidence

**Who is left behind.** Anciano, Lemanski, Culwick Fatti and Rubin (2026) find that "citizens in both low- and high-income areas are increasingly providing their own services", and that "state substitution is increasingly a daily act for many, not only in low-income settlements but also among elites" [IC: peer-reviewed]. They conclude that the practice raises "questions regarding the nature of the state and the social contract" [IC]. Scheba (2022) records a trend of leniency towards businesses and institutions that fail to pay municipal accounts while poor households face disconnection, citing Dugard (2016) [IC: peer-reviewed]. *Inference:* where self-provision follows the ability to pay, the wealthier parts of a town can secure services that its poorer parts cannot.

**Rates boycotts and withheld rates.** In October 2023 the Durban High Court found against the Westville Ratepayers Association in its dispute with eThekwini over disconnections linked to a rates boycott [IC], and eThekwini won a further ruling against the association in May 2025 [IC]. A 2011 ruling elsewhere was reported as "a blow to 'boycott' by residents"; it was not read in full [TBV]. The W2DF's own recovery concept leaves out rates withholding, because withheld rates would lower the collection rate the recovery plan depends on and expose residents to credit control [MS]. Theewaterskloof's own 2016 record listed "the Rates boycott and the poor collection rate in Genadendal" as its first issue to resolve [MS], and its recovery plan records that the Genadendal community "feels that the land does not belong to the municipality" [IC].

**Private enclaves and "semigration".** The prompt asks for evidence on gated estates and migration within South Africa by better-off households. No source on this was read in this run. It is listed in Section 9.

**The state offloading its duties.** MacLeod and Emejulu (2014) argue, from Scotland, that asset-based community development can serve as "neoliberalism with a community face", shifting responsibility to communities while resources are withdrawn [IC: peer-reviewed]. "Responsibilisation" is the term criminology and social policy use for this shift of responsibility from the state to individuals and groups (Garland, 1996) [IC]. In South Africa, McDonald and Ruiters (2005), working with the Municipal Services Project, documented the commodification of water through cost recovery and private contracts [IC: academic book]. Scheba (2022) argues that the municipal self-financing model itself produces inequality in water access [IC].

**Labour.** SAMWU told Parliament in March 2025 that it believed Mafube's electricity contract "was fraudulently drafted and signed" [IC]; no court has found this. IMATU raised job security and late salaries [IC]. Equal Education and SADTU argued in court that giving operating partners half the seats on collaboration school governing bodies weakens parents' say [IC].

**How parties have framed these cases.** The only cross-party record found is the NCOP committee's visit to Mafube on 18 March 2025 [IC]. The EFF called for Rural Maintenance to be removed as the electricity supplier [IC]. The DA welcomed the intervention and said the recovery plan "was based on inaccurate information on electricity and contract with Rural Maintenance" [IC]. The FF Plus raised the finance department and revenue collection of 10% to 20% [IC]. The ANC noted progress under the interventions and asked for a review of how local government is funded [IC]. The Mayor linked the collapse in part to "the privatisation of electricity" [IC]. AfriForum, which is not a party, frames community self-provision as a "fourth sphere of government" [MS]. Party manifestos for the 4 November 2026 elections were not read in this run.

## 5. The trend internationally

Each paragraph gives what the arrangement is, where and since when, who holds the function, its legal basis, the evidence on results, and how close it is to the four South African cases. Statements about the content of well-known books and articles are tagged [IC] where the publication itself is the record.

### 5.1 Comparisons close to the four cases

**Co-production and polycentric governance (Ostrom).** Elinor Ostrom defined co-production as the process by which inputs to a good or service come from people who are not in the organisation that delivers it, and argued from cases in Brazil and Nigeria that citizens and officials together can produce services neither produces well alone (Ostrom, 1996) [IC]. Polycentric governance, a term from Vincent Ostrom, Tiebout and Warren (1961), describes many centres of decision-making that are formally independent and work within a shared set of rules [IC]. Ostrom's commons work set out design principles for groups that manage shared resources over long periods, among them clear boundaries, rules that fit local conditions, monitoring by users and low-cost ways to resolve disputes [IC]. The evidence comes from many field studies, collected in *Governing the Commons* (1990) [IC]. Closeness: high for the language of the cases. Bonnievale's partnership with the province fits co-production; the leiwater of Greyton, with turns and volunteer water bailiffs [MS], fits the commons principles. The theory does not tell a group when to take a function from a failing state.

**Community-led local development and LEADER (European Union).** LEADER began in 1991 [IC]. Local action groups of public, private and civil society members write a local strategy for their area and choose projects funded by the EU and national governments [IC]. "Community-led local development" is the EU's name for the method when used in rural, urban and coastal areas [IC]. Planned funding for 2014 to 2020 was up to €9.2 billion [IC]. The European Court of Auditors concluded in 2022 that "there is little evidence to suggest that the benefits of the LEADER approach outweigh the costs and risks involved", and that local action groups are often not representative [IC]. Closeness: high in method. A local action group resembles a ward forum with a strategy. The difference is that LEADER groups receive a budget through a legal channel; none of the four South African cases does.

**Asset-based community development (ABCD).** John Kretzmann and John McKnight's *Building Communities from the Inside Out* (1993) asks communities to start from what they have, mapping the skills of residents, local associations and institutions, before naming needs [IC]. The asset-mapping step matches hypothesis step 1. Evidence on outcomes is mostly practitioner case studies; Mathie and Cunningham (2003) set out its use as a strategy for community-driven development [IC]. MacLeod and Emejulu (2014) argue it can be used to justify withdrawing state resources [IC]. Closeness: high for step 1, which none of the three outside cases followed.

**Collective impact and the backbone organisation.** Kania and Kramer (2011) described five conditions for groups of organisations working on one problem: a common agenda, shared measurement, mutually reinforcing activities, continuous communication and a backbone organisation with its own staff [IC]. Tom Wolff (2016) criticised the model for leaving out community leadership and policy change, and for its top-down origins [IC]. Closeness: moderate. The W2DF, WDI and the Ranyaka note's "thin shared professional layer" each describe something like a backbone [MS]. None of the three outside cases has paid backbone staff on record.

**Big Local (England).** Local Trust awarded £1 million to each of 150 neighbourhoods, each spending it over about fifteen years under a residents' partnership [IC: Local Trust]. By July 2025, 100 areas had completed the programme; it formally ended in March 2026 [MS: Local Trust]. Independent evaluations exist, including health research by Teesside University, but none was read in full for this run [TBV]. Closeness: high in scale and method. Big Local gives residents money and a long horizon with no state function attached.

**Community asset transfer and the Localism Act 2011 (England).** The Act gave community groups a right to nominate "assets of community value" and to bid when they are sold [IC]. Community asset transfer is the transfer of a building or land, usually from a council, to a community body [IC]. Closeness: moderate. It moves assets more often than services.

**Scotland's community right to buy and the Community Empowerment (Scotland) Act 2015.** The Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 created a community right to buy land [IC]. The 2015 Act added asset transfer requests to public bodies [IC]. A Scottish Government review of March 2025 said it "has not been possible to provide detailed quantitative breakdowns on asset transfer activity" [IC]; the *Community Ownership in Scotland 2023* report counted 840 assets owned by 533 community groups [IC]. Closeness: high for land. It is the nearest international parallel to the Farm 39 question, because it gives a statutory path for a community body to hold land, which TRANCRAA also provides and which has stalled at Genadendal [IC].

**The Preston model of community wealth building (England).** From about 2013 Preston City Council and the Centre for Local Economic Strategies worked with local "anchor institutions" (the council, a university, hospitals, a police force) to spend more of their budgets locally [MS: council and partners]. Partners reported £112.3 million of anchor spending retained in Preston and £448.7 million in Lancashire, up £74 million and £200 million from 2012/13 [MS]. No independent evaluation showing cause was found [TBV]. Closeness: moderate. It moves money, and no function. It matches the W2DF priority of local procurement [MS].

**Schönau and German energy co-operatives.** After Chernobyl in 1986, residents of Schönau in the Black Forest formed an action group, founded Elektrizitätswerke Schönau (EWS) in 1994, won two local referendums, raised money nationwide and took over the town's grid in July 1997 [IC: Centre for Public Impact and EWS]. EWS now has more than 6,000 co-operative members [MS]. Closeness: high for Mafube, with a difference in who holds the grid. In Schönau a citizens' co-operative bought the grid; in Mafube a private company operates the municipality's network under contract [IC].

**Rekommunalisierung (Germany).** In September 2013, 50.9% of Hamburg's voters supported buying back the city's energy grids, and the city then bought Vattenfall's 74.9% stake in the electricity grid company [IC]. A similar Berlin referendum in November 2013 failed to reach its quorum [IC]. Germany has more recorded cases of services returning to public ownership than any other country [IC: TNI]. Closeness: moderate. It shows the opposite direction to Mafube: from private operator back to a municipality.

**The Transnational Institute's remunicipalisation data.** *Reclaiming Public Services* (2017) recorded more than 800 cases [IC]. *The Future is Public* (2020) recorded 1,408 cases from 2000 to 2019 in more than 2,400 cities in 58 countries: 924 remunicipalisations of privatised services and 484 new municipal services [IC]. Energy (374) and water (311) lead [IC]. Germany (411), the United States (230), France (156), Spain (119) and the United Kingdom (110) have the most [IC]. The reasons recorded include failed private contracts, lower costs, lower user fees and democratic control [IC]. TNI is an advocacy research institute, and its database counts cases that partners report to it [MS]. Closeness: high as counter-evidence. It shows that functions move back to the state as well as away from it.

**United States: community development corporations, business improvement districts and Main Street America.** Community development corporations are non-profit bodies governed partly by residents, building housing and running services in low-income neighbourhoods since the 1960s [IC]. Business improvement districts, like South Africa's special rating areas, charge property owners an extra levy for local services; the first was in Toronto in 1970 [IC]. Main Street America, started by the National Trust for Historic Preservation in 1980, works with small-town business districts through a volunteer-led method [IC]. Closeness: high for special rating areas and for Mafube's levy.

**Australia and Canada: place-based collective impact.** Logan Together, in Logan, Queensland, began in 2015 as a collective impact initiative on early childhood [TBV]. Vibrant Communities ran in more than a dozen Canadian communities on poverty reduction, with an evaluation covering 2002 to 2010 [IC: CCEDNet, Maytree]. The evaluation summary read for this run gives no method or comparison groups [MS]. Closeness: moderate. Both are city-scale backbones with state funding partners.

**OECD and EU place-based policy (the Barca report).** Fabrizio Barca's independent report for the European Commission, *An Agenda for a Reformed Cohesion Policy* (2009), argued for a "place-based" approach in which outside funding is tied to local knowledge and local institutions, with external conditions to stop local elites capturing it [IC]. Closeness: high in idea. Barca names elite capture as the central risk, which bears on who sits at each case's table (Section 3.2).

### 5.2 Comparisons mentioned only

- **Machizukuri (Japan).** "Town-making": resident-led neighbourhood planning since the 1960s, often with municipal ordinances [TBV]. Close in spirit; set in a high-capacity state.
- **Kerala's People's Plan Campaign (India).** From August 1996 Kerala devolved 35% to 40% of state plan funds to local governments, with village assemblies (grama sabhas) setting priorities [IC]. A government evaluation found service projects had impact, productive projects had little, and many households never attended an assembly [IC]. Close as a model where the money follows the function.
- **Dana Desa (Indonesia).** Under the Village Law of 2014, central government pays a fund directly to nearly 74,000 villages, more than 20 trillion rupiah in 2015 [IC]. Critics noted weak accountability and allocations that ignored poverty [IC].
- **Umuganda (Rwanda).** Monthly compulsory community work, organised by the state [TBV]. State-led, so far from the four cases.
- **Harambee (Kenya).** "Pulling together": community fundraising for schools and clinics, promoted after independence in 1963 [TBV]. Close to Bonnievale's bricks, in a different political setting.

### 5.3 Which way does the evidence run?

The evidence shows movement in both directions, and the direction appears to depend on a few conditions.

**Towards places.** Steady movement of functions or money to places appears where a statute and a budget go together: Kerala's devolved plan funds [IC], Indonesia's village fund [IC], Scotland's rights to buy and to request transfers [IC], and LEADER's local action groups [IC]. In each, the state still sets the rules and supplies most of the money. The ECA's finding on LEADER [IC] and Kerala's evaluation [IC] show that the results are mixed even then.

**Back to the state.** Movement back to public ownership appears where private contracts expired or failed and a capable municipality could take the service back: Germany's grids [IC], and the 924 remunicipalisations TNI records, mostly in Europe and North America [IC].

**Conditions that seem to decide the direction.** *Inference*, from the cases above and the South African record:

1. **The capacity of the state.** Where the municipality can run a service, functions tend to move back to it when contracts end. Where it cannot, functions move to whoever can pay or organise: a private operator (Mafube), a trust with a provincial partner (Bonnievale), or self-help (Clarens).
2. **Whether a legal channel exists with money attached.** Scotland and Kerala have one; South Africa has sections 76 to 81 of the Systems Act and special rating areas, which de Visser describes as costly and slow [IC].
3. **Contract timing.** Remunicipalisation clusters at the end of concessions [IC: TNI]. Mafube's 25-year contract, signed in 2011 [IC], would run to about 2036 (*Inference*).
4. **Political coalitions.** Hamburg's grid returned by referendum [IC]. In Mafube, parties and unions disagree in public about the contract [IC].

**The TIMN lens.** David Ronfeldt's *Tribes, Institutions, Markets, Networks* (RAND, 1996) proposes that societies add forms of organisation over time: the tribe (kinship and identity), the hierarchical institution, the market and, most recently, the network [IC]. On this reading the MBF, WDI and the W2DF are network forms: voluntary, cross-sector and without command authority [MS]. The evidence supports one part of the lens. Each network form in the cases depends on the other forms to act: on courts and a province (institutions), on a private operator or donors (markets), and on the identity of a place or a congregation (the tribal form) [MS]. No case shows a network form replacing an institution. *Inference:* TIMN fits the cases as a description of a new form working beside the older ones. The cases do not show it as a sequence in which the network displaces the state.

### 6.2 Regenesis Group's Story of Place

Regenesis Group, whose principals have included Pamela Mang, Ben Haggard and Bill Reed [TBV], develops a "Story of Place" at the start of each project: a study of how a place's ecological patterns gave rise to its cultural patterns, used to find what the place is uniquely able to become [MS: Regenesis and Volans case study]. Mang and Reed set out the method in "Designing from place: a regenerative framework and methodology" (*Building Research & Information*, 2012) [IC], and Mang and Haggard in *Regenerative Development and Design* (Wiley, 2016) [IC]. The method asks designers and communities to understand a place's essence and history before proposing anything, which matches Michael Haupt's claim that the operating system cannot be refined before the origin story is understood [MS].

Where it has been used: the Volans case study names Rio Sabinal in Mexico, where the story brought out the river's cultural and spiritual meaning, and a beachfront project at Viña del Mar in Chile, where it was used to reshape a contested development with community groups [MS]. Neither account gives years or measured results [MS]. A separate body, The Regenesis Project in the Philippines, applies a "Story of Place" to Bohol and Cebu City; its founder's June 2026 article proposes that the method should "build an 'operating system' for long-term change", including place-specific economies, zoning and education [MS]. A 2025 review in *Sustainable Development* maps the conceptual base of regenerative development [IC]; this run did not find an independent evaluation of Story of Place outcomes.

*Inference:* Story of Place is the closest existing method to the Origin Story half of Place OS, and at least one practitioner already pairs it with the words "operating system". It differs from Place OS in two ways. It starts from ecology and designs projects; it does not deal with the transfer of state functions. And it treats the story as one story of the place, where the Valley of Grace record shows two or more stories told by different communities (Section 7).

### 6.3 Marshall Ganz's public narrative

Marshall Ganz, at the Harvard Kennedy School, teaches public narrative as three linked stories: a story of self (why I am called), a story of us (what we share) and a story of now (the urgent challenge and the choice) [IC]. He sets it out in "Public Narrative, Collective Action, and Power" (2011) [IC]. It is used to train organisers and was used in the 2008 Obama campaign [TBV]. Evidence on outcomes comes mainly from practitioners [TBV]. *Inference:* public narrative fits the founding moment (layer two) closely. Each case has a "story of now": school places in Bonnievale, the clean-up in Villiers, mine closure in the Waterberg. It is a method for a founding group, and it says little about the deep story of a place.

### 6.4 Place identity and narrative in local development

Environmental psychology uses "place identity" for the part of a person's identity tied to places (Proshansky, Fabian and Kaminoff, 1983) [IC]. In planning, Leonie Sandercock (2003) argued that stories are central to planning practice, used to persuade, to include and to exclude [IC]. Edward Relph's *Place and Placelessness* (1976) described how places lose their distinct identity [IC]. *Inference:* this literature supports the idea that a place's story affects who feels they belong to an initiative. It does not show that a better-understood story produces a better-run operating system.

*Inference:* the three outside cases record a founding moment and no deep story. Their founding moments are "stories of now" in Ganz's sense: a deficit, a crisis or a closure. The Valley of Grace is the only case with a recorded deep story, and its founding moment is plural.

### 7.2 Versions of the Valley of Grace's story

The prompt asks for Genadendal and Greyton to be described only through their own published statements. Community-authored documents are rare, as Michael Haupt notes [MS]. The versions found are set out below, each attributed. The W2DF register's own pages are listed last, as the register's version.

**Genadendal, in its own published words.**

- *Isaac Balie, former director and curator of the Genadendal Mission Museum, in* Genadendal, The Valley of Grace: A Long Walk Through the History of the First Mission Station in South Africa *(2020; 2023 edition).* The book tells the village's story from 1737 through its church, schools, buildings, crafts and people [MS]. It records the 1858 land grant and says many residents rejected it because no resident was made a trustee [MS], and it says many residents left the Moravian Church over the 1909 Act [MS]. It names Genadendal's firsts: the first juvenile school outside Cape Town, the first solid bridge, the oldest fire engine and the first professional magazine for teachers [MS].
- *Judith Balie, custodian of the Mission Museum, in a Change Exchange interview (2026).* "Every artefact and building has a story to tell"; "You still feel the presence of everyone who had an impact on this town"; "We want to go out into the community and share those untold stories about everyday people" [MS]. The museum is running an oral memory project [MS].
- *genadendal.info, a local information site whose operator is not named.* "The Genadendal Mission Station was initially established on 23 April 1738"; "A story of people from different backgrounds coming together" [MS].
- *The Ward 2 Forum (2016 to 2019), in its study* Farm 39 Transformation: The Lost 18 Years *(2017).* It argued that "the requirements of the community, and not those of TWKM, should be paramount", and that the Transformasie Komitee had properly represented the community [MS].
- *Genadendal participants and the Transformasie Komitee at the W2DF's first meeting.* They raised "the longstanding nature of the process" and "the ability of the community to utilise its land and associated resources effectively", and said affected communities "should be involved from the beginning of proposals" [MS].

**Greyton, in its own published words.**

- *Greyton Tourism, the village's tourism bureau.* "In 1846 a wealthy Englishman, Herbert Vigne, bought Weltevreden"; the village was named "after Sir George Grey, the then Governor of the Cape"; "During the 1950s the town was torn apart by the implementation of the Group Areas Act" [MS]. The page names the Hessequa near the Gobos River and their leader Stoffel Koekson [MS].
- *Greyton Transition Town and Greyton Today.* Local histories of Weltevreden, the Vigne brothers, the leiwater and the commonage [MS], which the register notes "often share one origin" with the tourism sources [MS].
- *Keith Radmore, president of the Rotary Club of Greyton and a great-great-grandson of Herbert Vigne.* He keeps the Vigne family papers and is digitising them [MS]. He gives 1844 for Herbert Vigne's voyage on the *Athenian*, where the register gives 1842 [TBV].

**Heuwelkroon.** No account written by a Heuwelkroon residents' body was found. The nearest is an essay in the arts journal *Herri*, by a Cape Town writer who came to Greyton in 2017 and spoke with residents: before the removals residents "lived together irrespective of 'race'", and afterwards "a community of subsistence farmers who owned their own farms were overnight reduced to having to work as gardeners and domestics for white people" [MS]. The register's Greyton page lists "the stories of Heuwelkroon, told by the families who were moved there" as still open [MS].

**The first peoples.** No account by descendants of the Soaqua, Chainoqua or Hessequa was found. The register records that "who speaks for them" is for the Forum to agree [MS].

**Outside voices.** President Mandela named his Cape Town residence Genadendal in 1995 and described the village as "a place of sanctuary for more than a thousand slaves when slavery was abolished in 1838" [IC for the naming; MS for the quotation]. Theewaterskloof's recovery plan describes Genadendal in terms of billing: "services are rendered and billed, but the community feels that the land does not belong to the municipality" [IC].

**The register's own version.** *History of the Valley* and *History of Greyton* tell both stories in date order, tag each claim, and list where the sources disagree, including the founding date (1737 or 1738), the land grant (1856 or 1858) and the dates of the removals (the 1950s, or 1968 to 1974) [MS]. *What Began Here* reads the record as a sequence of things the valley started, things taken from it and what "carried it through" [MS]. These pages are written by the Forum's convenor from published sources [MS].

*Inference, without taking a side:* the versions differ in four places. They differ in where the story starts (a pear tree in 1738, or a farm bought in 1846). They differ in what the land story is about (a trust for residents that was never completed, or a freehold village whose coloured owners were removed). They differ in who is the main actor (the mission and its residents, or a founder and his descendants). And they differ in how the state appears (as the body that closed the college and has not transferred Farm 39, or as a council that ran Greyton from 1910). Each version is told by different people, and the same events carry different weight in each.

### 7.3 What the evidence says about origin stories and outcomes

**From the cases.** None of the three outside cases records its deep story, so the cases cannot show whether understanding it changes how an initiative works. Their founding stories do appear to matter to the founders. Bonnievale's trust lists unity as its first lesson [MS]; its relations manager says "it worked because there was really the whole town involved" [MS]. These are practitioners' claims.

**From the methods.** Regenesis presents Story of Place as the basis for better design and for "re-knitting" a community [MS]; no independent outcome evidence was found (Section 6.2). Public narrative is taught as a way to move people to act [IC]; evidence on outcomes comes mainly from practitioners [TBV]. The place identity literature shows that people's identities are tied to places [IC] and that stories in planning can include and exclude [IC].

**On who joins and who is trusted.** The Valley of Grace record shows the story affecting who is trusted on land. The Forum agreed to leave the TRANCRAA process to "other platforms" [MS], sends anything touching Farm 39 to the Transformasie Komitee first, with 21 days to respond [MS], and records that Genadendal participants asked to be involved "from the beginning" [MS]. *Inference:* the deep story (a trust for residents since 1858, a 1909 Act that denied ownership, a transfer stalled since 2002) sets limits on what an outside convenor can propose about land, and in what order.

**Where a story appears to have helped.** Bonnievale's account of a town that built its own school in six months is the story its funders and the press repeat [MS], and the model is being copied in other towns [MS on most; Struisbaai's facility IC]. *Inference:* a founding story with a clear deficit and a visible result travels, and appears to draw outside money.

**Where a story appears to have shut people out.** The valley's record shows the 1858 grant excluding residents from the trusteeship [MS], the 1909 Act excluding them from ownership [IC], and the Group Areas Act removing coloured families from Greyton [IC]. In Mafube, the case page records no channel for township residents who are not in business [MS], while separate township bodies spoke to Parliament on their own [IC]. *Inference:* a founding story told by business owners and farmers can leave others outside the initiative even when they share its goals.

**Separating evidence from claims.** Nothing found in this run measures whether an initiative that understands its place's deep story lasts longer, keeps more members or draws more investment. Michael Haupt's claim that the operating system cannot be refined until the origin story is understood [MS] remains untested.

## 8. What the evidence implies for Place OS

Everything in this section is *Inference*, tied to the patterns and sources named in each point. It describes parties only through what they have said or done, and makes no proposal about any named party.

### 8.1 Components a Place OS would need, if the patterns hold

1. **A trigger the place recognises.** Each outside case began from a deficit, crisis or closure that residents could see (Section 3.1, step 1). The Valley of Grace has several on record: the recovery plan's cash limits [IC], the stalled Farm 39 transfer [IC], one Afrikaans high school for the valley [IC]. A Place OS would need to name which of these it answers first.
2. **A small core with a plan for succession.** All four cases depend on a few named people (Section 3.2). The valley's two earlier forums stopped [MS]. A Place OS would need a way to hand on the convening role.
3. **One coherence device that people can point to.** Bonnievale's school did this; Mafube's unsigned Compact did not (Sections 2 and 3.2). A Forum with five priorities is a device for talking; the cases suggest a flagship with a visible early result draws outsiders.
4. **A legal vehicle matched to the function.** A trust as operating partner fitted a public school (Bonnievale) [IC]; a forum without legal form cannot hold land, money or a service delivery agreement. Sections 76 to 81 of the Systems Act allow a community body to deliver a municipal service, at the cost of a slow process [IC].
5. **An agreement with the sphere that holds the function.** The one clear transfer among the cases (Bonnievale) had the province as a willing partner [MS]. Mafube's Compact failed at the point of government's signature [IC]. For the valley, education sits with the province, land on Farm 39 with the national Minister, and water and electricity reticulation with the municipality [IC: Constitution, Schedules 4 and 5; TRANCRAA].
6. **Its own money, accounted for in public.** No case publishes accounts (Section 3.2). The Ranyaka note's R100 Resource Journey offers a way to show where money goes [MS].
7. **A channel for people outside business and farming.** Only Bonnievale's governing body gives parents a set place [IC]. The valley's community protocol [MS] and the Komitee's 21-day review [MS] are the beginnings of such a channel.
8. **A record that separates claims from evidence.** Every case's results are self-reported (Section 2). A Place OS that tags its own claims, as the register does, would answer the question funders raise about evidence (for Ranyaka's note, the cost and localisation claims are listed as hypotheses to test [MS]).

### 8.2 Steps the evidence does not support, or supports only in some settings

- **Scanning before action.** No outside case shows it (Section 3.1). The valley may be the first.
- **Transfer as the normal outcome.** One of three cases shows a clear transfer, and that one is co-provision with a province. The national policy direction in 2026 moves functions to utilities and agents [IC].
- **Origin as a tested cause of success.** Untested in every case and in the methods literature (Sections 6 and 7).
- **A one-way trend.** Internationally, functions also move back to municipalities, with 924 recorded remunicipalisations from 2000 to 2019 [IC: TNI].

### 8.3 Where transfer went badly, and what was done about it

- **Mafube.** The electricity concession is defended by the MBF and the operator [MS] and disputed by the Mayor, SALGA, SAMWU, the EFF and the Namahadi Business Forum [IC]. National Treasury's plan proposes reassessing its legality [IC]. The operator went to court over the plan, and the court ordered Treasury to hand over its record [IC]. Municipal revenue collection was reported at 20% in March 2025 [IC]. What was done: litigation on both sides, and a parliamentary recommendation that the Mayor meet the business forums every quarter and meet the province and the operator about the contract [IC].
- **Koster.** A court order letting residents and AfriForum's company run water and sewage plants was reversed, and the water board resumed control [MS].
- **Makana.** A dissolution order was replaced by a court-supervised recovery plan with quarterly reports [IC]. Activists faced personal danger, as Scheba (2022) records [IC].
- **Collaboration schools.** The model was challenged in court over parents' say, and upheld [IC].
- **Internationally.** Germany's grids returned to municipal hands when concessions ended [IC]; the ECA found LEADER's added value unproven [IC].

Each of the South African examples ended in court. The one with a statute behind it, the collaboration school model, survived the challenge [IC].

### 8.4 Where the Valley of Grace differs from the three cases

- **Mission-station land and TRANCRAA.** The Remainder of Farm 39 is held by the national Minister in trust [IC], and the municipality's spatial plan says it may not invest in infrastructure on land it does not own [IC]. None of the three outside cases has land held in this way. Any Place OS function that needs land on Farm 39 depends on a process the Forum has agreed to leave to "other platforms" [MS].
- **Two communities with distinct histories.** Genadendal and Greyton tell different deep stories (Section 7.2). The three outside cases each tell one founding story.
- **A municipality under intervention, with a recovery plan.** Mafube shares this [IC], and its experience shows recovery plans and private contracts colliding [IC]. Theewaterskloof's plan limits every budget and tariff until the intervention ends [IC].
- **A forum convened with municipal support.** Mafube's forum confronted its municipality and WDI works beside its municipalities without agreement; the W2DF began with the municipality's Economic Development office as co-convenor [IC].
- **Outside interest before any project.** Ranyaka's note names Genadendal/Greyton as a possible demonstration place [MS], and the Places Conversation guests agreed to visit [MS]. In Bonnievale outsiders came after the school was built [MS].
- **The 2038 tricentenary.** No outside case has a fixed date in its future that its deep story supplies. The Forum has set its long-term goal against 23 April 2038 [MS].
