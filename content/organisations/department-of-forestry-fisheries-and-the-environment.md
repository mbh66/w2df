The Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE) is the national government department responsible for the environment, forestry, and fisheries [IC]. It publishes the national list of threatened ecosystems, which since November 2022 has listed Greyton Shale Fynbos as Critically Endangered [IC]. It runs public works programmes that clear invasive plants, including Working for Water [MS]. It also plans how money for biodiversity is raised: from the national budget, from incentives that bring in private money, and from international funds such as the Global Environment Facility [IC].

## How it plans to raise money

### The Resource Mobilisation Action Plan

The department has a Resource Mobilisation Action Plan that identifies where environmental fiscal measures are needed, such as taxes, levies, and incentives [MS]. That is the only description of the plan found in public sources so far. Its text, its date, and how it divides domestic and international sources have not been found. The department's officer for the Global Environment Facility sits in a unit called International Governance and Resource Mobilisation [IC].

In 2022 the department, the [[organisations/south-african-national-biodiversity-institute|South African National Biodiversity Institute]], and the United Nations Development Programme's Biodiversity Finance Initiative (BIOFIN) published a map of "essential life support action areas" [MS]. The map shows where actions to protect nature, restore it, and meet climate and development goals overlap, so that money can be aimed at those places [MS]. It feeds into the Resource Mobilisation Action Plan [MS]. Its grid squares are one kilometre across, so it can guide regional planning but cannot decide questions about a single site [MS].

### The Biodiversity Finance Plan

The plan that sets out the finance options in most detail is the Biodiversity Finance Plan, prepared with BIOFIN in February 2018 [MS]. It estimated that the national biodiversity strategy needed about R63 billion over ten years [MS]. Government spent R11.54 billion on biodiversity in 2016 [MS].

The plan put forward 16 ways to close the gap, in three groups [MS]:

- **Protected areas.** Making the case for funding, raising more of their own income, reforming property rates, tax incentives for landowners, rules for biodiversity offsets, revolving land trusts, and better-funded stewardship programmes.
- **Restoring ecosystems.** Drawing on existing government grants, scaling up incentives for land users, earning more from products made from cleared invasive wood, water tariffs that pay for catchments, carbon tax offsets, and global climate funds.
- **Sustainable use.** Making environmental fines work better, a Tourism Conservation Fund, and the national Biodiversity Economy Strategy.

Of the 16, the plan found that 11 depend mainly on private money from inside South Africa, and four on public money from inside the country [MS]. Only one, global climate funds, draws on international public money [MS].

### The draft national biodiversity plan, 2026 to 2035

The department published a draft of South Africa's National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan for 2026 to 2035 in the Government Gazette on 5 August 2026 [IC]. Several of its parts are still placeholders, among them the Minister's foreword and the executive summary [IC]. A new Biodiversity Finance Plan is being prepared alongside it [MS].

**Who pays for biodiversity now.** The draft says that the department and its public entities have paid for 61% of the country's biodiversity spending, other national departments for 7%, provinces for 22%, non-profit groups for 4%, and municipalities for 1% [MS]. Spending rose from R15.25 billion in 2016 to R20.14 billion in 2024, but once inflation is counted it fell, from R14.31 billion to R12.9 billion in 2016 rand [MS]. It is less than 1% of the government's consolidated budget [MS]. Private spending is the largest gap in the figures [MS].

**One target for money and skills.** The draft joins the two global targets on finance and on capacity into one national target: by 2035, the people and money needed to carry out the plan are secured, with money drawn from all sources and a suitably skilled workforce [MS]. Money is measured three ways: real growth in domestic public funding, real growth in private funding from inside and outside the country, and growing income from finance ideas that have been tested [MS]. The draft says the first two cannot be measured yet, because no system tags biodiversity spending in budgets [MS].

**The actions, and who leads them** [MS]

- Update and carry out the Biodiversity Finance Plan. Lead: the department.
- Build a system to tag biodiversity spending, linked to National Treasury's green finance taxonomy. Lead: National Treasury and the department.
- Direct income from water charges to protecting and restoring the most important catchments and wetlands. Lead: the [[organisations/department-of-water-and-sanitation|Department of Water and Sanitation]], with catchment management agencies and SANBI.
- Formal partnerships with banks and companies, and more use of green bonds, biodiversity offsets, and biodiversity credits, with safeguards for people and the environment. Lead: the department, with National Treasury.
- Long-term funding beyond project cycles, for example for protected areas after they are declared. Lead: the department.
- Budgets set aside within national and provincial allocations for biodiversity work led by communities. Lead: the department.
- Money from non-state and private sources to help reach 30% of land and sea protected by 2035. Lead: the department and National Treasury.

**What the plan costs.** A financial needs assessment costed 17 of the plan's 125 activities, at about R656.71 million over ten years [MS]. The draft says this is a partial figure, and that the costliest work is not yet costed, including large-scale restoration and growing the protected areas [MS].

**The new finance plan.** The draft says the new Biodiversity Finance Plan has four aims: bringing in private and blended finance, market-based incentives for conservation, tracking biodiversity in public budgets, and better governance of biodiversity finance [MS]. At its centre is a proposed National Biodiversity Blended Finance Facility, which would pool money from government, development banks, and private investors for restoration, stewardship, catchments, and the biodiversity economy [MS]. The instruments it lists are environmental fiscal benefits, budget tagging, biodiversity offset banking, thematic bonds, conservation trust funds, other green finance, environmental tariffs and fees, and crowdfunding and philanthropy [MS].

The draft does not mention the Resource Mobilisation Action Plan by name.

### Money from inside South Africa

- **The national budget.** The department received R9.08 billion for 2025/26 [MS]. The Minister said that this was a real decrease of 1.4% on the year before, once inflation is counted [MS]. The national biodiversity assessment of 2025 found that public spending on biodiversity is rising in rand but falling once inflation is counted [MS].
- **Public works.** In 2025/26 the department committed R2.869 billion to Working for Water, Working for Waste, Working for Fire, Working for the Coast, and Furntech [MS]. It reported more than 496,000 hectares of invasive plants cleared in 2024/25, and set a target of 326,081 hectares for 2025/26 [MS]. Daily Maverick reported in 2025 that funding for Working for Water and related restoration fell from R1.7 billion in 2020/21 to R377 million in 2024 [TBV].
- **Incentives for landowners.** Since 2016, a landowner who declares a nature reserve or national park on their land can deduct 4% of the land's value from taxable income each year for 25 years [MS]. UNDP says that adding land to the conservation estate through stewardship agreements can cost the state 17 times less per hectare than running a state protected area [MS].

### Money from outside South Africa

- **The Global Environment Facility.** The department's Director-General is South Africa's political focal point for the Global Environment Facility [IC]. The facility lists 33 national projects in South Africa, with total financing of about US$202 million [IC]. Its biodiversity allocation to South Africa for its eighth funding period was about US$35.7 million, all of it committed [IC]. The draft national biodiversity plan puts the facility's grants to South Africa across its recent funding periods at US$232.1 million [MS].
- **The United Nations Development Programme.** BIOFIN, a UNDP programme, has worked with the department on the finance plans and the action map [MS].
- **Climate funds.** The 2025 national biodiversity assessment names the Green Climate Fund as a source that could add to the Global Environment Facility [MS]. The draft national biodiversity plan says the Green Climate Fund approved US$40.1 million in 2025 for reducing disaster risk by working with ecosystems [MS].
- **Other governments.** The draft says that cooperation with several countries supports work on climate adaptation and the oceans, without naming them [MS].

### Money for waste

The National Waste Management Strategy of 2020 aims to divert 45% of waste from landfill within five years, 55% within ten, and at least 70% within fifteen [MS]. The draft national biodiversity plan sets a target that, by 2035, half of the waste that cannot be prevented is kept out of landfill through better sorting at source [MS]. It makes the department the lead on carrying out the waste strategy, which it describes in four pillars: the circular economy, effective waste services, capacity, and compliance [MS]. Rules on extended producer responsibility came into force in 2020, under which makers of packaging, electronics, lighting, pesticides, batteries, and lubricants pay towards dealing with their products once they are thrown away [MS]. A national Waste Bureau is funded from the levy on plastic [MS]. The department says local government pays for waste services mostly from rates, about 90%, with about 10% from national government [MS]. It is working with the Green Climate Fund and the End Plastic Alliance on studies of new ways to treat organic waste and plastics in six municipalities, and with the World Bank [MS].

## How its work reaches the valley

- **The valley's fynbos.** The department's national list of threatened ecosystems, published in November 2022, lists Greyton Shale Fynbos as Critically Endangered [IC]. Pages on this site cite that listing.
- **Clearing.** Working for Water is the department's main clearing programme [MS]. The draft national biodiversity plan sets a target of 10,639 square kilometres of invasive plants cleared nationally, and 13,497 square kilometres of fynbos restored, drawn from targets the department sent to the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification [MS]. It makes the department the lead on new ways to pay for restoration, such as bonds and credits for biodiversity, water, or carbon, and water tariffs [MS]. Whether it clears in the Riviersonderend mountains above the valley today is not known. The clearing that this site records there is done by [[economic/farmers-land-water/wild-restoration|Wild Restoration]] and by [[organisations/capenature|CapeNature]] [MS].
- **Finance for clearing.** The Biodiversity Finance Plan's ideas of water tariffs that pay for catchments, and of land trusts, are close to ideas raised on this site. The [[organisations/how-the-cape-water-bond-works|Cape Water bond]] is one example already running in the province. The department is not among the parties to the bond that this site lists.
