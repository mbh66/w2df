Cape Town gets most of its water from a group of dams in the mountains east of the city, linked by tunnels and pipes. Together they are called the Western Cape Water Supply System [IC]. The largest of these dams, Theewaterskloof, lies near Villiersdorp in Theewaterskloof Municipality, the same municipality as Ward 2 [IC]. Below the dam, the Riviersonderend flows east past Greyton and Genadendal, and the valley's own rivers join it [IC]. This page explains how the system works, who gets its water, and why it matters to the valley.

## The big dams

Six large dams hold almost all the system's water. Together they store about 898 million cubic metres when full [IC]. Some belong to the national [[organisations/department-of-water-and-sanitation|Department of Water and Sanitation]] and some to the [[organisations/city-of-cape-town|City of Cape Town]] [IC].

| Dam | Holds when full (million cubic metres) | Where | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Theewaterskloof | 480 [IC] | On the Riviersonderend, near Villiersdorp | Department of Water and Sanitation [IC] |
| Voëlvlei | About 160 to 170 [IC] | Near Gouda, filled by canals from the Klein Berg, Leeu and Vier-en-Twintig rivers [TBV] | Department of Water and Sanitation [IC] |
| Berg River | 130 [TBV] | On the Berg River, near Franschhoek. Finished in 2009 [TBV] | Not confirmed |
| Wemmershoek | 59 [IC] | On the Wemmershoek River, near Franschhoek | City of Cape Town [IC] |
| Steenbras Lower | 34 [IC] | On the Steenbras River, above Gordon's Bay | City of Cape Town [IC] |
| Steenbras Upper | 32 [IC] | Upstream of Steenbras Lower | City of Cape Town [IC] |

Sources give slightly different sizes for some dams. Voëlvlei differs most: 159, 164 and 172 million cubic metres in three sources [IC].

Theewaterskloof alone holds about half the water of the six big dams: 480 of 898 million cubic metres, or 53% [IC]. One source gives 41% [TBV]. Its earth wall is about 37.5 metres high [IC].

The City also owns eight small dams on Table Mountain, near Simon's Town and in the Helderberg [IC]. Together they hold less than 5 million cubic metres [IC]. The largest, Kleinplaats Dam near Simon's Town, holds about 1.4 million [IC].

## How Theewaterskloof's water reaches Cape Town

Theewaterskloof is the heart of the Riviersonderend-Berg River Government Water Scheme, the largest part of the system [IC]. The scheme joins three river basins that run in different directions: the Riviersonderend flows east, the Berg flows north, and the Eerste flows into False Bay [IC].

The scheme has three parts [IC]:

- **Theewaterskloof Dam,** filled by its own catchment and by winter water brought through the tunnels from rivers near Franschhoek and Stellenbosch.
- **A tunnel system** through the mountains, including the Franschhoekberg tunnel (12 kilometres) and the Stellenboschberg tunnel (5 kilometres).
- **Kleinplaas Dam,** a small dam on the Eerste River that controls the flow through the tunnels. It is a different dam from Kleinplaats Dam near Simon's Town.

In winter, water flows through the tunnels into Theewaterskloof. In summer, water is released back through the tunnels to farmers in the Berg and Eerste valleys, to towns such as Stellenbosch, and to Cape Town [IC]. The Department of Water and Sanitation runs the scheme and plans maintenance on it with the City [MS].

## Who gets the water

Theewaterskloof's water goes two ways [IC]:

- **West, through the tunnels,** to Cape Town, Stellenbosch and the Helderberg, and to farmers along the Berg and Eerste rivers.
- **East, down the Riviersonderend,** to farmers along the river and to the [[organisations/overberg-water-board|Overberg Water Board]], which supplies Caledon and other towns.

Two documents give yearly shares of the scheme's water. An older planning document, written before the Berg River Dam was built, gives rounded figures [IC]. The newest Department of Water and Sanitation report on the system, its Annual Status Report of November 2018, gives what each user was receiving at that time [IC].

| User | Older document (million cubic metres a year) | Department, 2018 (million cubic metres a year) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Farmers along the Berg River | 49 | 48.6 |
| Farmers along the Riviersonderend (Riviersonderend Irrigation Board) | 31 | 30.8 |
| Paarl and Wellington | 16 | Not listed |
| Vyeboom Irrigation Board (also spelled Vygeboom) and farmers who pump from the dam | 15 | 14.7 (13.2 and 1.5) |
| Stellenbosch Irrigation Board | 10 | 9.7 |
| Helderberg Irrigation Board | 8 | 7.8 |
| Overberg Water Board | 4 | Named, with no figure |
| Farmers along the Eerste River | 3 | 2.8 |
| Banhoek Irrigation Board | 2 | 1.8 |
| Stellenbosch Municipality | Not listed | 3 |
| City of Cape Town | The rest | 118 to the Faure and Blackheath treatment works, and 0 to 23 through a pipeline to Wemmershoek |

The 2018 report takes its diagram of the scheme from a 2006 Department document [IC]. Several of its figures differ very little from the older ones, so some may have been carried over without being measured again.

The same report also lists allocations, the volumes users are entitled to. These can differ from what users receive [IC].

- **City of Cape Town:** 118 million cubic metres a year from Theewaterskloof. Of this, 90 is a fixed allocation and 28 a temporary one, first lent to the City in 1989 from farming's share while farmers were not using it [IC]. Since 2003, 10.86 million of farming's unused share has been licensed to farming projects the report describes as 100% black economic empowerment (BEE) projects [IC].
- **Zonderend Irrigation Board,** the farmers along the Riviersonderend: 36.1 allocated, against a need of 41.5 [IC].
- **Vyeboom Irrigation Board and farmers who pump from the dam:** 29.5 allocated [IC].
- **Overberg Water Board:** 4.0, the figure used in the Department's planning model [IC].
- **All farming in the system:** about 198.6 allocated in 2018, up from 162 set in the 2003 agreement for the Berg River Dam [IC].

Across the whole system, farms used about a third of the water and smaller towns about 5% in 2009 [TBV]. The system irrigates about 15,000 hectares of farmland [TBV].

The system is short of water. Provincial and national officials say it is over-allocated: even with full dams there may not be enough for every user [MS]. In the 2018 drought, the farmers along the Riviersonderend received only 60% of their allocation [IC].

Each year the Department reviews the water in the system at an annual operating analysis and decides whether restrictions are needed [MS]. The 2026 review was set for 2 November 2026 [MS].

## A new dam planned in the Overberg

The Western Cape Government lists a proposed Overberg Dam among its priority infrastructure projects [IC]. It would hold about 123 million cubic metres on the Breede River near Swellendam, with feasibility studies planned for 2027 and 2028 [MS]. It is planned as a water project for the Overberg District, and nothing published so far links it to Cape Town's supply [TBV]. See [[concepts/provincial-pipeline|Provincial Pipeline]].

## Why it matters to the valley

### The dam is in our municipality

The dam shares its name with the municipality, and it belongs to the national government [IC]. Decisions about how its water is shared are made by the Department of Water and Sanitation and the [[organisations/breede-olifants-catchment-management-agency|Breede-Olifants Catchment Management Agency]] [MS].

### The valley lives downstream

The Gobos River from Greyton and the Baviaans River from Genadendal join the Riviersonderend below the dam [IC]. The water that farmers along the river receive is a mix of what the dam releases and what streams like these add [IC]. The river below the dam also has its own needs, set in law as the Reserve (see [[rules/national-water-act|National Water Act]]) [IC].

### Cape Town pays for healthy catchments

Since 2018 the [[organisations/greater-cape-town-water-fund|Greater Cape Town Water Fund]] has cleared invasive plants from the mountains that feed Cape Town's dams [MS]. Since April 2026 the Cape Water bond has paid for part of that work, with a bonus to lenders only when the clearing is checked [IC]. See [[organisations/how-the-cape-water-bond-works|How the Cape Water Bond Works]].

### A place-based economy

The Forum reads the system as part of what a place-based economy for the valley could be built on. Cape Town and the farms of the Berg and Overberg depend on water that falls on the same mountains as the valley. Water the valley's land gives, measured and proved, is something the system already needs. Three ideas tabled at the Forum build on this: [[concepts/from-water-access-to-water-agency|From Water Access to Water Agency]], [[concepts/restoring-the-river|Restoring the River]], and [[concepts/ridge-to-river-to-table|Ridge to River to Table]].

## Following the dam levels

- The Department of Water and Sanitation publishes a weekly report on the state of the country's dams [IC].
- The City of Cape Town publishes its dam levels daily, and a weekly water dashboard [MS].
- [Cape Town Dam Levels](https://capetowndamlevels.co.za/data-sources/) gathers these figures in one place. Its operator describes it as a personal side project [MS].

## What is still open

- **Current shares.** The newest Department of Water and Sanitation report with yearly shares is from November 2018. No later report with shares has been found, including from the annual operating analysis.
- **Overberg Water Board's share.** The 2018 report names the Overberg Water Board as a user below the dam, but gives no current volume for it.
- **Sizes and owners.** Who owns the Berg River Dam, and which size for Voëlvlei is current.
- **Releases for the river.** Whether Theewaterskloof releases water to meet the Reserve below the dam today.
- **The Overberg Dam.** Whether it would ever be joined to this system.
- **Maintenance.** How often the tunnels are shut for maintenance, and how that affects releases down the Riviersonderend.
