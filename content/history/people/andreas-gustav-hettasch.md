Andreas Gustav Hettasch was a Moravian missionary who served at Genadendal from 1859 to 1864, and again from 1877 to 1894 [MS]. In his second term the village's crafts were failing, and he tried new work to give people a living [MS]. He led the building of the church that stands on Church Square today, which is sometimes called the Hettasch Church [MS].

## His life

[[history/genadendal-a-long-walk|Balie's history]] says Hettasch was of Roman Catholic origin, and committed his life to Christ at the age of 21 [MS]. He was appointed to Genadendal again in 1877 [MS]. The mission was then in a hard state: 600 adults had been put out of the church for failing to pay their contributions [MS]. Balie's history says he won the confidence of the community, quietly restored their membership, and that a spiritual awakening broke out among the children in 1885 [MS].

## New work for the village

By the late 1800s factory goods were undercutting the village's crafts, and many people left to find work [MS]. Hettasch started projects to create jobs: 13,000 castor oil plants were sown, and 12,000 mulberry trees planted to feed silkworms for raw silk [MS]. He encouraged residents to collect buchu and everlasting flowers for export to Europe [MS]. An oil press came from England, and Genadendal's castor oil won prizes at the Agricultural Exhibition in Cape Town in 1886 [MS]. For the Colonial and Indian Exhibition in London in 1886, he listed 33 medicinal herbs used in the valley, where they grew, and how they were used; the collection won an honourable mention [MS]. He also planted hops and tobacco, which Balie's history says many Christians thought a mistake [MS]. None of the projects lasted [MS].

## The church on Church Square

In 1889 the congregation decided to replace the church of 1800, and started a building fund [MS]. Residents raised 1,225 pounds, the mission society gave 700 pounds, the old church's materials fetched 519 pounds, and gifts from abroad came to 656 pounds [MS]. The last service in the old church was held on 20 August 1891 [MS]. Hettasch chose the Italian Renaissance (palazzo) style [MS]. Men made the bricks, and women and children carried about 5,000 a day to the site [MS]. On 15 March 1893 he unlocked the doors of the new church with the words of Psalm 100:4, "Enter his gates with thanksgiving" [MS]. It is also called the Jubilee Church, because it marked a hundred years since the missionaries of 1792 [MS].

His wife, Agnes Paulina Hettasch, born Ballein at Berthelsdorf in 1838, died at Genadendal on 23 April 1894, and he returned to Europe after her death [MS]. Their son Otto Gerhard Hettasch, born at Clarkson in 1871, later served as a missionary in the Himalayas and at Genadendal [MS].

## His legacy in the valley

- The church he built is still in use on Church Square [MS].
- The museum keeps the castor oil press and silkworm cocoons from his projects [MS].
