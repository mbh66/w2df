On 24 December 1792, three Moravian missionaries arrived at Baviaanskloof: Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn, and Johann Christian Kühnel [IC]. It was 48 years since [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] had been forced to leave [IC]. They found [[history/people/magdalena|Magdalena]] still keeping his New Testament [MS]. Like Schmidt, they were craftsmen who earned their own living [MS]. Within a few years they had reopened the school and built a chapel, a forge, and a water mill, and the mission grew into Genadendal [IC]. This page covers all three, because they arrived, worked, and wrote their diaries together.

## Why they came

After Schmidt left in 1744, the VOC and the Dutch Reformed Church at the Cape would not allow Moravian missionaries to return [IC]. Moravian sources say the church leaders in Europe, working through the Moravian settlement at Zeist in the Netherlands, spent years seeking permission [MS]. The details of how it was won rest on Moravian sources alone [MS]. Permission came in 1792 [IC].

The three men were chosen as Herrnhut chose its missionaries: tradesmen who would support themselves and live among the people they served [MS]. Marsveld was a tailor, Schwinn a shoemaker, and Kühnel a knife-maker [MS].

## Reopening the mission

The three arrived on 24 December 1792 and found the ruins of Schmidt's house and the pear tree still standing [MS]. They found Magdalena, about 80 and nearly blind, with the New Testament Schmidt had given her [MS].

People came quickly. Eight kraals of Khoekhoe moved to the kloof, and formal lessons began on 4 March 1793 with 25 adults [MS]. The school has run ever since, and is now [[lr-schmidt-primary|L.R. Schmidt Primary School]] [MS].

The work was contested from the start. Colonists in the Overberg depended on Khoekhoe labour and did not want the mission to draw workers away from their farms [IC]. The historian Russel Viljoen has written about this conflict in the years 1792 to 1795 [IC]. The VOC would not let the missionaries build a church [IC]. They could build only after the British took the Cape in 1795 [IC].

Then the mission grew fast:

- A chapel was consecrated in 1796 [IC].
- In March 1797 they built a forge, and a water mill at about the same time [IC]. Kühnel used the forge to make knives, which became known across the Cape [MS].
- A larger church was finished by 1800 [IC].
- In 1806 the governor renamed the mission Genadendal, "valley of grace" [IC]. More than 1,000 Khoekhoe were living there by then [MS].

## The Genadendal Diaries

The three kept a diary of daily life at the mission. It records who came, what was taught, who fell ill, and how the missionaries dealt with farmers and officials [IC]. The first volume, covering 1792 to 1794, was translated into English and published in 1992 by the University of the Western Cape as *The Genadendal Diaries* [IC]. It is one of the main sources for the valley's history, and for the life of Magdalena in her last years [IC]. Like Schmidt's diary, it shows the Khoekhoe through the missionaries' eyes.

## Their legacy in the valley

- Kühnel House, in the mission complex on Church Square, is a Provincial Heritage Site [IC].
- The water mill, the forge, and the school they started are part of the story the [[genadendal-mission-museum|Genadendal Mission Museum]] tells today [MS].
- Genadendal knives, made in the tradition Kühnel began, are in the museum's collection [MS].
