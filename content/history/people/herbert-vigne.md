Herbert Vigne was an English settler who bought the farm Weltevreden in 1846 and laid out a village on it, which he named Greyton [IC]. Greyton's histories call him the father of Greyton [MS]. He came to the valley a century after [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]], and his village grew up beside the mission settlements. Its story is part of the history of the whole valley.

## His life

Vigne was the son of a London merchant whose family descended from Huguenots, French Protestants who had fled to England [TBV]. He came to the Cape in 1842 with his older brother, Henry Vigne (1805 to 1881), who farmed Tygerhoek near present-day Riviersonderend [TBV]. The family held Tygerhoek for about 120 years [TBV]. Greyton histories say the brothers fell out, and that Herbert had to leave Tygerhoek [TBV].

Helen Robinson's 1986 study of Greyton, which drew on family papers held by his descendants, adds detail [TBV]. It says he was the sixth of nine surviving children of Henry and Mary-Anne Vigne, and that his elder brother Henry Thomas Vigne became member of Parliament for Caledon in 1854 [TBV]. Another brother, George, married Anna Grey, the sister of Sir George Grey, the Governor after whom Greyton was named [TBV]. The family records Robinson saw say the brothers did not agree on farming at Tygerhoek [TBV].

In 1846 he bought Weltevreden [IC]. Some sources give 1845 [IC]. Robinson dates the transfer to 21 December 1846, and says he had moved onto the farm by 1847 [TBV]. She says he served for some years as field-cornet of the district [TBV]. A Greyton history says he first settled on a farm at Boschmanskloof [TBV]. Weltevreden had been granted to [[history/people/marthinus-theunissen|Marthinus Theunissen]] in 1793, on land that Greyton's histories say had been held by the Khoekhoe leader [[history/people/stoffel-koekson|Stoffel Koekson]] [IC]. It later passed to [[history/people/hendrik-cloete|Hendrik Cloete]] of Groot Constantia in 1795, and in 1839 to [[history/people/john-malcolm-stewart|John Malcolm Stewart]], its first British owner [IC].

In the 1860s he married [[history/people/elizabeth-vigne|Elizabeth Belshaw]], who was 27 years younger than him [TBV]. They had three sons and a daughter [TBV]. Robinson says he stayed a bachelor until he was over forty, that Elizabeth was the daughter of James Belshaw, one of the buyers of his plots, and that they had five children [TBV]. He died in 1895 [IC], and Elizabeth died four months later [TBV]. After his death his heirs divided his farm in the village, De Bos, and Greyton went through hard years [TBV]. Robinson calls the home farm "The Bush", and says the heirs went on selling the remaining land until about 1910 [TBV]. The gravestones of Herbert and Elizabeth Vigne were later moved to the grounds of St Andrew's Anglican Church in Greyton [MS].

## Founding Greyton

Vigne laid out a village on Weltevreden. Sources date its founding to 1852, with the first plots sold in 1854 [IC]. He named it after Sir George Grey, the Governor of the Cape [IC]. The surveyor [[history/people/j-g-rietz|J.G. Rietz]] drew the town plan [IC]. Robinson quotes the conditions of sale, dated 27 and 28 December 1854, and found that at least 120 plots were sold outright that year, for between £5 and £11 10s each [TBV]. She writes that he went on selling plots in batches of twenty or thirty for the rest of his life [TBV]. In 1863 he was allowed £800 on a tender for a footpath through the Boesmanskloof to Lady Grey, now McGregor [TBV]. In 1864 he invited the Genadendal missionaries to hold services in Greyton's English school [TBV].

South African History Online says the plots came with title deeds, water rights, and grazing rights, and were sold to buyers of any race, nationality, or religion [TBV]. It calls Greyton the only town in the Cape where such land was for sale to anyone [TBV]. This claim matters for the valley's later history, because a century later the Group Areas Act forced coloured families out of Greyton to [[villages/heuwelkroon|Heuwelkroon]] [IC]. It has not been checked against the deeds.

Greyton's histories say Vigne kept two small portions for himself and left the rest of the farm to the owners of the plots as a commonage, shared land for grazing [TBV]. The same wording appears in many sources, which suggests they share one origin [MS]. His will or deed of gift has not been found [TBV]. The conditions of sale of 1854, as Robinson quotes them, cede the rest of his estate of 1,886 morgen as a town commonage, to "remain forever inalienably apportioned" to it, and keep 10 morgen in the Magermanskloof for himself [TBV]. Part of the commonage is now the [[economic/farmers-land-water/greyton-nature-reserve|Greyton Nature Reserve]], proclaimed on 13 May 1977 [IC].

## Links to other figures

- [[history/people/stoffel-koekson|Stoffel Koekson]]'s people are said to have held the land before it became Weltevreden, and to have settled at Boschmanskloof, where Vigne first lived [TBV].
- [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] started the mission at Genadendal 116 years before Greyton's first plots were sold.

## What is still open

- Whether the conditions of sale of 1854, as Robinson quotes them, set the full terms of the commonage. A copy from the Deeds Office would confirm the wording.
- Whether the first plots were open to buyers of every race, as Greyton sources say. The first title deeds would show this.
- His exact dates of birth and death.
- How many children he had. Greyton's histories say four; Robinson says five.
- His links with Genadendal. Robinson records his invitation to the missionaries in 1864, and that some Genadendal residents bought plots. The mission's day-books may hold more.
