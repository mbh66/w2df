Christian Ignatius Latrobe was an English clergyman of the Moravian Church, and a painter and composer [IC]. In 1815 his church sent him to the Cape to visit its mission stations, and he spent much of the next year at Genadendal [IC]. While he was there, he and the missionaries drew up rules for the village, which its residents accepted as a "Brotherly Agreement" [MS]. His *Journal of a Visit to South Africa in 1815 and 1816*, published in London in 1818 with his own coloured illustrations, is one of the fullest accounts of Genadendal in those years [IC].

## His life

Latrobe was born at Fulneck, a Moravian settlement in Yorkshire, and was ordained in the Moravian Church in 1784 [IC]. He worked for the church's missions for most of his life [IC]. He was also a composer and a friend of Joseph Haydn, and he edited a six-volume *Selection of Sacred Music* that brought works by Haydn, Mozart, and others to English audiences [IC].

## At Genadendal

Latrobe reached the Cape in 1815 and visited the missions at Genadendal and Groenekloof (Mamre) [IC]. From Genadendal he travelled east through George and Uitenhage to the Great Fish River, and planned a new mission station, Enon, on the Witrivier near Kirkwood [IC]. [[history/genadendal-a-long-walk|Balie's history]] says he made the journey to Algoa Bay in an ox-wagon built at Genadendal, which is now in the mission museum [MS].

During his visit, rules for the residents were drawn up and agreed by all [MS]. They were meant as an agreement between brothers, and did not have the force of law [MS]. Among them: that anyone who wished to live at Genadendal would follow the teaching of Jesus and his apostles; that residents would keep their persons, houses, and surroundings clean; that everyone would stay at home in the evening; and that no one would bring strong drink into the village [MS].

He wrote about what he saw. He noted that the missionaries spoke a Dutch so mixed with German that both a German and a Dutchman could understand it, and called it "Genadendal Dutch" [MS]. He described the church of 1800, the funerals of the Khoekhoe residents, and the gardens, whose appearance, he wrote, "betrays the disposition" of each gardener [MS]. He met the Khoekhoe captain Koopman, who told him that the grazing land given to the church and two neighbouring farmers left his people too little for large herds [MS]. When a Hessequa deputation objected that the missionaries had named a captain from the Koopman clan, Latrobe told them that as one congregation they should rejoice in Christ, "the only Captain of our Salvation", whatever tribe or nation they belonged to [MS].

He painted Genadendal and its buildings, and his pictures are among the earliest views of the village [MS]. When he left, he wrote that he had never felt a keener pang on leaving any place [MS]. On 20 June 1828 a portrait of Latrobe, sent from London, was unveiled at Genadendal, and it now hangs in the museum [MS].

## His legacy in the valley

- The rules of 1816 shaped life in the village for generations [MS].
- His journal and paintings are among the main sources for Genadendal in the 1810s [IC].
- The ox-wagon and his portrait are in the [[cultural/sport-heritage-arts-culture/genadendal-mission-museum|Genadendal Mission Museum]] [MS].

## What is still open

- How the residents saw the rules of 1816, and whether any record of their own survives.
- Whether the wagon in the museum is the one he travelled in. Balie's history gives two accounts of who built it.
