Vehettge Tikkuie was a Khoekhoe woman from the Sonderend River valley [MS]. In 1742 Georg Schmidt baptised her with the name Magdalena [IC]. When Schmidt was forced to leave the Cape in 1744, she kept his small congregation together for 48 years, with no missionary, no church building, and no pay [IC]. When new missionaries arrived in 1792, she was still teaching [MS]. Without her, the story of Genadendal might have ended in 1744. She is also remembered as Moeder Lena [IC].

## Her life

Vehettge was born in the early 1700s to Khoekhoe parents who herded livestock and moved with the seasons, in the area of the Sonderend River and Sergeants River [MS]. She married [[history/people/janneke-tikkuie|Janneke (also called Jantjie) Tikkuie]] according to Khoekhoe custom [MS]. The Moravian Church in South Africa says she was Hessequa and her husband Chainoqua [MS]. Janneke helped Schmidt by fetching supplies and hunting to feed the community [MS].

She met Schmidt in 1737, and when he moved to Baviaanskloof in April 1738, she and her husband went with him [MS]. Schmidt taught in Dutch, and she became one of his four best students, with [[history/people/africo|Africo]], Kupido, and Willem [MS]. Schmidt's diary also shows that she was her own person. In February 1739, angry at quarrels in the community, she told him she would not stay any longer, and left for five days before coming back [MS].

In 1742 Schmidt received the authority to baptise, and he baptised her with the name Magdalena [IC]. The Moravian Church in South Africa gives the date as 4 April 1742, after he had baptised three men [MS]. She was the first woman he baptised [MS]. [[history/people/christina|Christina]] was baptised the same year. Her husband was not baptised with her [MS].

## The 48 years (1744 to 1792)

Schmidt left the Cape in 1744 [IC]. The mission broke up, and Magdalena went back to Sergeants River [MS]. She kept the Dutch New Testament Schmidt had given her, wrapped in a sheepskin [MS].

She gathered the people who remained under the pear tree in Schmidt's garden, prayed with them, and read to them from the New Testament [IC]. A resident later told the missionaries: "Every evening we all, men, women and children would go to old Lena. Then she would fall on her knees and pray" [MS]. By 1775 and 1776, European travellers at the Cape had heard of the Khoekhoe woman who prayed and read the Bible [MS].

She did this for 48 years, without a church, a salary, or any support from outside [IC].

## The return of the missionaries

Three Moravian missionaries arrived at Baviaanskloof on 24 December 1792 [IC]. They found Magdalena, by then about 80 and nearly blind, still keeping Schmidt's New Testament [MS]. The missionary Hendrik Marsveld recorded that she came to support them, attended the school lessons, and helped students who were struggling [MS]. In 1794 she fell seriously ill during an outbreak of sickness, and recovered [MS]. Visitors to the Cape, among them Lady Anne Barnard, came to meet her [MS].

She died on 3 January 1800 [IC], shortly before the mission's new church was consecrated [MS].

## How she is remembered

- Historians have written her story, including Henry Bredekamp (1987), Christina Landman (1996 and 1998), and J.A. Millard (1999) [IC].
- She is honoured by the Moravian Church in South Africa, including during Women's Month [MS].
- Schmidt's first mission is often told as his story. Her 48 years show that the Khoekhoe side of the meeting of 1738 carried it through its hardest time. See [[future-of-the-valley|Future of the Valley]].

## What is still open

- The year of her birth, and her age when she died.
- Whether she was Hessequa, as the Moravian Church in South Africa says.
- Whether she had children, and whether any families in the valley descend from her.
- Where exactly she lived at Sergeants River between 1744 and 1792.
- What she herself thought and said, in her own words. Everything recorded about her was written by missionaries.
