Johann Christian Kühnel was one of the three Moravian missionaries who reopened the mission at Baviaanskloof, now Genadendal, on 24 December 1792 [IC]. He was a knife-maker, or cutler, and the youngest of the three [MS]. After years of waiting for leave to build, he set up the mission's forge in 1797, and the knives he made there were soon sold across the Cape [IC]. The forge gave the mission its first trade for sale, and taught the craft to Khoekhoe apprentices. His story is also told, with that of Hendrik Marsveld and Daniel Schwinn, on [[history/people/missionaries-of-1792|The Missionaries of 1792]].

## A craftsman missionary

Moravian missionaries were chosen from among tradesmen, who were expected to earn their own living among the people they served [MS]. Kühnel was a German knife-maker [MS]. The Dutch copy of the diaries calls him and Schwinn "two German brothers", who in the first months could not yet preach in Dutch [MS]. He brought files with him from Amsterdam, and the church in Europe sent him lathes, grindstones, and screw clamps, so that he could earn money towards the mission's costs [MS].

## The first years (1792 to 1795)

The three missionaries arrived on 24 December 1792 [IC]. In February 1793 Schwinn and Kühnel moved into the first room of the new mission house, built of clay by Khoekhoe helpers [MS]. In April 1793 the missionaries made a bell from a sheet of brass bought at the Cape, because the pupils had no clock and had to be called to school [MS]. A study of the Moravian mission bells credits Kühnel with making it [MS].

Kühnel shared the daily work of the mission: teaching, holding the singing meetings, visiting the sick, and leading Khoekhoe work parties to cut wood in the mountains [MS]. On 27 December 1794 he held the baptism of a woman who took the name Catharina [MS]. In April 1794 he rode east with [[history/people/hendrik-marsveld|Hendrik Marsveld]] and a Khoekhoe man, Jacob, to Swellendam and the Snake River, probably the Slangrivier near present-day Heidelberg, to visit Khoekhoe kraals [MS].

### A workshop forbidden

The mission needed a workshop for Kühnel's trade, and its foundation was laid in 1794 [MS]. Commissioner [[history/people/abraham-josias-sluysken|Abraham Josias Sluysken]] ordered that nothing be built until the Commissioners-General returned from Batavia [MS]. The missionaries wrote that if they built, they could be called disobedient, and "might perhaps give the whole mission a blow" [MS]. In January 1795 a senior officer in Cape Town, Colonel Gordon, asked Marsveld whether Kühnel had started forging knives. Marsveld answered that they had no workshop, because the Commissioner had forbidden them to build "anything at all, even a goat shed" [MS].

## The crisis of 1795

In March 1795 Juffrouw Teunis, of the company post at Soetemelksvlei, sent for one of the missionaries because Khoekhoe cattle had got into her garden. Kühnel rode to her, and told her the missionaries could not help it and she must do as she thought best. She had the cattle impounded, and their owners paid 2 shillings a head to get them back [MS].

On the night of 29 July 1795, when the order of [[history/people/bisani|Commandant Bisani]] reached the mission, Kühnel rode to [[history/people/baas-teunis|Baas Teunis]] for advice. Teunis told him to obey, because "might goes before right", and offered to keep the mission's goods safe [MS]. Earlier that month Kühnel had ridden to the farms of [[history/people/gildenhuijs|Gildenheuser]] and Mary for news of the rebels [MS]. After the missionaries returned from Cape Town, he arranged with Baas Teunis in September to share out the government's flour among 116 wives and children of Khoekhoe men serving at the Cape [MS].

## The forge (1796 to 1797)

Under British rule the mission could build. In December 1795 Kühnel rode to buy boards and to show Baas Teunis the new permit to cut building timber [MS]. In July 1796 the overseer of the warm baths made him a pair of bellows, a tool so dear that some farmers had paid 40 or 50 rixdollars for one [MS]. In October 1796 the missionaries decided to build a workshop for him [MS]. In March 1797 he and Marsveld drove to Cape Town for the tools the smithy needed [MS].

The forge was built in March 1797 [IC]. The farmers round about had agreed among themselves not to buy anything the mission made [MS]. Before the smithy was even finished, they were ordering knives, and by the end of 1797 Kühnel could not make a quarter of what was wanted [MS]. He took a young Khoekhoe man as an apprentice [MS]. The knives became known as Genadendal knives and were sold across the Cape [MS].

## Later years

Kühnel went on teaching as well as working the forge. In May 1797, when children misbehaved in school, he told them he could not speak to them of the Saviour and sent them home. The children crowded round him and begged forgiveness, and he spoke with them and forgave them [MS]. In August 1797 he fetched sheep from a farmer who had bought them on a journey into the Xhosa country, giving the mission a flock of its own for the first time [MS].

In December 1803 Kühnel and Brother Rose rode with some Khoekhoe to the boundary of the mission's land to welcome the Commissioner-General, De Mist [MS]. Kühnel signed the mission's diary in February 1804, with Rose, Kohrhammer, Marsveld, and Schwinn [MS]. When and where he died has not yet been found.

## How to read his story

What is known of Kühnel comes almost entirely from the diaries he and his fellow missionaries wrote for their church in Europe. They record what the missionaries did and thought. The young Khoekhoe man who learned the trade at his forge is not named, and nothing is recorded of how the Khoekhoe residents saw him.

## His legacy in the valley

- Kühnel House, in the mission complex on Church Square, is a Provincial Heritage Site [IC].
- Genadendal knives, made in the tradition Kühnel began, are in the collection of the [[genadendal-mission-museum|Genadendal Mission Museum]] [MS].
- The forge began the mission's crafts for sale. In the second half of the 1800s factory goods undercut them, and many people left to find work elsewhere [TBV]. See [[history/markets/mission-economy|The Mission Economy]].

## What is still open

- Where and when he was born, and when and where he died.
- Whether he married, and what became of him after 1804.
- Whether Kühnel House was his home, and when it was built.
- The name of his Khoekhoe apprentice, and whether the craft passed down in Genadendal families.
- What Genadendal knives sold for, and where.
