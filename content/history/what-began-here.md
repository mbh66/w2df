The valley's history is difficult to read in some places. Khoekhoe land was taken, a missionary was expelled, residents were denied ownership of the land they lived on, a college was closed, and families were forced from their homes. Yet this small valley started things that spread across South Africa, and many of them are still here. This page sets out what began here, what was taken, and what carried the valley through. It then shows how the plans for 2038 continue the same story. For the full account, see [[history/history-of-the-valley|History of the Valley]] and [[history/history-of-greyton|History of Greyton]].

## What was taken

The things that began here grew in spite of what was done to the valley:

- **Land.** Khoekhoe grazing land was taken for colonial farms from the 1600s onwards [IC]. See [[history/people/soeswa|Soeswa]] and [[history/people/stoffel-koekson|Stoffel Koekson]].
- **The first mission.** Schmidt was forced to leave in 1744, after the Cape's ministers rejected his right to baptise [IC].
- **Ownership.** The law of 1909 let residents live on the mission land but not own it [IC]. More than a century later, the transfer of Farm 39 is still not complete [IC]. See [[trancraa-process-farm-39|TRANCRAA and Farm 39]].
- **Trades.** Cheap factory goods undercut the valley's crafts in the second half of the 1800s, and many people left to find work [TBV].
- **Higher learning.** In 1926 the government closed the teachers' college, arguing that coloured people were suited to farm work and did not need higher education [IC].
- **Homes.** Under the Group Areas Act, coloured families were forced out of Greyton to Heuwelkroon, probably between 1968 and 1974 [TBV].

## What carried it through

Each time the valley's institutions were taken away, something smaller kept going.

The Moravian Church itself had survived this way. After it was banned in 1620, families kept its faith and its books in secret for about a hundred years, until refugees renewed the church at Herrnhut in 1727 [IC]. Its bishop [[history/people/jan-amos-comenius|Jan Amos Comenius]] had prayed that a "Hidden Seed" would survive [MS]. [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] was born in one of the villages where it did [MS].

The same thing happened in this valley. When Schmidt left in 1744, the valley was left with no missionary and no church building. For 48 years [[history/people/magdalena|Magdalena]] gathered people under the pear tree and read to them from the New Testament he had left her [IC]. When the missionaries returned in 1792, the congregation was waiting [MS].

What carried the valley through was held by households and passed on at home: faith, reading, crafts, music, and knowledge of the land. Laws could take the land and close the college. They could not take what people had learned. The school that began in 1738 has been teaching since 1792 [MS]. In Greyton, residents still keep the leiwater running, as volunteer bailiffs and street wardens [MS].

## The learning arc

Learning runs through the valley's story more than anything else.

| When | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 1738 | Schmidt's school under the pear tree [IC] |
| 1792 | The school reopens, and has run ever since [MS] |
| 1838 | South Africa's first teachers' training college opens [IC] |
| 1926 | The government closes the college [IC] |
| Today | The valley has one high school, [[emil-weder-secondary\|Emil Weder Secondary]], which teaches in Afrikaans [IC]. There is no place of study after school [TBV]. |
| 2028 | Families of children starting Grade 8 choose high schools, with help from the [High School Decision Tool](https://hs.valleyofgrace.co.za) [MS] |
| 2030s | The proposed [[concepts/valley-of-grace-learning-campus\|Valley of Grace Learning Campus]], with a Bioregional Learning Centre, would bring study after school back to the valley |

Seen this way, the Learning Campus is the next step in a 300-year story. The first school taught reading, and the college trained teachers. The campus would train young people for work in the valley's own land, water, and heritage, and bring back the higher learning that was taken in 1926. Emil Weder would remain the valley's Afrikaans high school, with its own heritage and governing body.

## The wider arc

The valley's story has always reached beyond the valley. The mission belonged to a church with missions on several continents [IC], and the words printed at Genadendal fed the growth of a language [IC]. In 1995 the valley's name was chosen for the president's residence [IC].

The next reach is already under way. Places around the world are building local economies around their own rivers and land. One network, the Design School for Regenerating Earth, says it aims to help start 1,000 bioregional learning centres by 2035 [MS]. The Forum's plan for the valley is called the BioConomy: running more of the local economy so that what the valley has stays in the valley, including money, skills, water in the ground, and healthy soil [MS]. The BioConomy framework was first worked out in this valley [MS]. See [BioConomy](https://bioconomy.earth).

The Forum's research describes this as the valley moving from being needy to being needed [MS]. The valley would once again have something to teach: how a place with a hard history can rebuild its economy around its own land, water, and people.

## Towards 2038

On 23 April 2038 the valley marks 300 years since the meeting at Baviaanskloof. The things that began here were made by very different people working things out together, often on unequal terms. The plans for 2038 ask whether a second meeting, on fairer terms, can start the next 300 years. See [[future-of-the-valley|Future of the Valley]] and [[long-term-goal|Our Long-Term Goal]].
