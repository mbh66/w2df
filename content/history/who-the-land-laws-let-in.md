Every land law draws a line. On one side are the people it lets live on the land, own it or speak about it. On the other are the people it leaves out. This page follows where that line fell in Greyton and in Genadendal, from the 1858 grant of [[villages/farm-39|Farm 39]] to the Transformation of Certain Rural Areas Act of 1998 (TRANCRAA).

## The five questions every land law answers

1. **Who's in and who's out?** Race, church, where you lived on a certain day, a permit, or membership of a body.
2. **Who draws the line?** The state, a church, a board, the Minister, or residents by vote.
3. **Who has a say?** Who can object, vote, appeal or go to court.
4. **What happens to people outside the line?** Removal, a permit, quiet tolerance, or protection under another law.
5. **Is the land on hold?** Does the waiting end, and can the land carry title, a loan or services while it waits?

## Greyton: a line drawn by race

Greyton's plots were sold from 1854 to buyers of any colour, and families classified "coloured" owned land in the village for more than a century [TBV]. "Coloured" and "White" were the apartheid state's categories. This page uses them only where a law or a source does.

In 1964 or 1965 a committee of the Group Areas Board sat in the district. Ratepayers classified "coloured" sent it a petition asking to stay on their land [TBV]. Nobody had a right to be heard by that committee. It chose whom it would hear [TBV].

On 26 July 1968 the State President signed Proclamation 216 of 1968 under section 23 of the Group Areas Act 36 of 1966. The *Government Gazette* published it on 9 August 1968 [IC]. It made most of Greyton an area "for occupation and ownership by members of the White group" [IC]. A smaller strip along Market Street and the road to Caledon, up to Erf 595, went to the "Coloured group" [IC]. Some Greyton histories say the removals happened in the 1950s, and Helen Robinson's study gives 1969 for the official announcement [TBV]. The Gazette shows the line was drawn in 1968 [IC].

Robinson found about 55 families classified "coloured" who owned property in Greyton in 1969 and 1970. Between them they held more than 80 erven, almost half the property owners in the village [TBV]. People who rented were moved first [TBV]. At least 50 properties were sold under the Act from 1969 to 1974, and Robinson traced 66 that passed to white owners by 1986 [TBV]. Heirs often sold cheaply to buyers from outside the valley [TBV].

Most families went to Heuwelkroon. Its 100 houses were all full by 1975 [TBV]. Robinson records that the relocation site was made on Greyton's commonage, and that old servitudes over the commonage and its water were cancelled when the village boundaries moved [TBV]. The white area was extended twice, on 25 June 1973 and 25 June 1976 [TBV]. Some families bought plots on Genadendal land at Boschmanskloof, where the Genadendal Management Board supplied electricity and sanitation. Others left for Cape Town or other towns [TBV].

Eight families stayed in the village. In 1977 they were told to apply for permits to remain. They refused [TBV]. They were then allowed to stay and farm for the owner's lifetime only. When three owners died in 1979 and 1980, their wives and sons were "disqualified" from inheriting, and the properties were sold [TBV].

In 1991 the Abolition of Racially Based Land Measures Act 108 of 1991 ended race as a basis for owning land [IC]. Since then anyone may buy land in Greyton under ordinary title. The [[history/history-of-greyton|History of Greyton]] tells the rest of this story.

## Genadendal: a line drawn by residence

The register quotes the 1858 grant as speaking of people "from time to time lawfully resident" on Farm 39 [MS]. Nobody has yet found the deed itself to check those words [TBV]. A Moravian mission journal of 1921 says the land was acquired by government grant [MS].

"From time to time" describes a list that moves. A person joins it by coming to live there lawfully and leaves it by going away. What made residence lawful would have depended on whoever ran the station.

From 1909, a Cape law put mission stations under boards chaired by the magistrate [IC]. The Mission Stations and Communal Reserves Act 29 of 1909 recognised owners of land granted under section 8 and people it called "registered occupiers" [IC]. Selling, leasing or bonding a plot needed the Governor-General's approval (section 9(1)) [IC]. An amendment of 1929 let the Governor-General cancel the "right of residence" of a person who owed money and was neither an owner nor a registered occupier, after three months' notice. Anyone who then stayed was treated as "an idle and disorderly person" (section 19, as amended by Act 12 of 1929) [IC]. Whether a proclamation applied this Act to Genadendal by name hasn't been found [TBV].

The register records that Proclamation 98 of 1924 put a secular board in charge of Genadendal [TBV]. Its text hasn't been found. In 1961 the Coloured Persons Communal Reserves Act 3 of 1961 applied the 1909 Act to "coloured persons settlement areas". Its text doesn't name Genadendal [IC]. Which law governed Genadendal from 1961 to 1989 is still not known [TBV].

The Rural Areas Act 9 of 1987 placed "trust land" in the Minister's name (section 7) [IC]. In 2014 the Land Claims Court named this Act and TRANCRAA as the laws that apply to "rural areas such as Genadendal" [IC]. The register records that Genadendal was added to the Act in 1989. The notice hasn't been found [TBV].

Under all of these laws, the line ran around residence on mission land. The state and its boards decided who stayed, and no law found gave residents a vote on it.

## Where TRANCRAA draws its line

TRANCRAA was assented to on 20 October 1998 and came into force on 2 November 1998 [IC]. It abolished rules that tied land rights in these areas to "a particular population or ethnic group" (section 5(1)(c)) [IC].

Section 1 defines a "resident" as a person who, "at the date of commencement of this Act", ordinarily lived in the area or had to pay rates, rent or service charges there [IC]. The date is 2 November 1998. Nobody has found an amendment that changes it [TBV].

This list doesn't move. It's a picture of one day.

People on the list who are 18 or older may elect a committee [IC]. The municipality, or the elected committee, recommends who should receive the land, and the Minister decides (section 3) [IC]. A person the Minister appoints to investigate may "hear or receive representations from any person" (section 3(9)(b)) [IC].

People born on Farm 39 after 2 November 1998, people who married into a household after that date, and people who settled there after it, Madiba Park among them, aren't "residents" under section 1 [IC]. Section 3(2) still says no transfer may happen unless the Minister is satisfied that the receiving body's rules balance the rights of residents, members and "present and future users or occupiers of land" [IC]. No court or official has been found to say what that means for people who came after 1998. No published list of residents for Farm 39 has been found either.

The Act meant the waiting to be short. Section 9 sets a transitional period of 18 months, which the Minister may extend by six [IC]. For Farm 39 it began on 8 July 2009 and ended in January 2011 with no transfer [IC]. Land not transferred by then "vests in the Minister, who may continue to hold such land in trust" (section 3(13)) [IC]. The Act gives no end date after that.

The words "illegal occupant" and "unlawful occupier" don't appear in TRANCRAA [IC]. The 1909 Act spoke of a "right of residence" and a "registered occupier" [IC]. "Unlawful occupier" comes from the Prevention of Illegal Eviction from and Unlawful Occupation of Land Act 19 of 1998 (PIE). It means a person who occupies land "without the express or tacit consent of the owner or person in charge, or without any other right in law", and it leaves out people that other tenure laws protect [IC]. On land the Minister holds in trust, whether a person is an unlawful occupier turns on consent. That is a question for a lawyer.

## Questions residents ask

### Who may speak about the Farm 39 process?

Anyone. TRANCRAA lets residents vote for a committee, and it lets the Minister's investigator hear representations from "any person" (section 3(9)(b)) [IC]. Section 16 of the Constitution protects freedom of expression, within the limits in section 16(2) and section 36 [IC]. The view that only the Act's beneficiaries may speak isn't supported by the law. A court ruling would settle it.

### Can people who settled on Farm 39 after 1998 benefit from its land?

The Act's text keeps them off the list of residents, so they don't vote for the committee [IC]. That part of the view is supported. The same Act tells the Minister to be satisfied that the receiving body's rules protect "present and future users or occupiers of land" before any transfer (section 3(2)) [IC]. Other laws reach them too. PIE requires a court order before anyone is evicted, and the Interim Protection of Informal Land Rights Act requires consent before a person loses an informal right to land [IC]. The view that they can't benefit is partly supported. The Minister's decision on the receiving body's rules, or a legal opinion, would settle it.

### Does forming a co-operative affect the land process?

A co-operative needs at least five members and a constitution lodged with the registrar (Co-operatives Act 14 of 2005, sections 6 and 7) [IC]. It needs no land. Registering one gives its members no right to Farm 39 land [IC]. The view that helping people form co-operatives interferes with the land process isn't supported.

Using land is a separate question. A settlement between the Minister, the municipality and the Komitee was made an order of court on 17 October 2008, and a moratorium was placed on the sale of the trust land [IC]. The Minister holds the remainder of Farm 39 (section 3(13)) [IC]. Section 2 vests trust land in the townships in the municipality, subject to rights already registered there [IC]. The DevForum's own co-operative pages say any use of land on the mission settlements goes to the Transformasie Komitee first [MS]. No DevForum page records a co-operative that has been registered or has used Farm 39 land [MS]. A legal opinion on any particular use of land would settle what is allowed.

### Does freedom of expression cover comment on TRANCRAA?

Yes. Section 16(1) of the Constitution protects it, subject to section 16(2) and section 36 [IC]. The view is supported.

### Do sections 59 and 72 of the Constitution give a say here?

Section 59 covers the National Assembly and section 72 the National Council of Provinces [IC]. They give the public a way into Parliament's work. They don't speak to a Minister's decision under TRANCRAA. For the municipality's part, Chapter 4 of the [[rules/municipal-systems-act|Municipal Systems Act]] sets out how residents take part [IC]. The view is partly supported. A legal opinion would settle how far each reaches.

## What the evidence shows

Before looking at the evidence, the DevForum set out three things it could show:

1. **The pattern repeats.** TRANCRAA draws its line from outside, people outside it have no say, and the land waits with no end.
2. **The line changes, the waiting stays.** TRANCRAA draws its line on a new basis and lets residents choose, but the land still waits.
3. **A break.** TRANCRAA changes both the line and the waiting, and what keeps Farm 39 stuck lies somewhere else.

On the line, the evidence points to the second. TRANCRAA replaced race with residence on one date, and it gives residents a vote. The text is clear on this. The evidence is moderate overall, because no court has yet said how section 3(2) applies to people who came after 1998.

On the waiting, the evidence sits between the first and the second. The Act lets the Minister hold untransferred land with no end date [IC], and Farm 39 has waited since 1998. But other areas have moved under the same Act. The Haarlem CPA received its deed in February 2020, and land at Enon and Bersheba passed to the Witrivier CPA by October 2023 [IC]. That suggests the delay on Farm 39 comes partly from how the Act has been carried out.

Some evidence points the other way. The fixed date closes the list of voters. The Minister keeps the final word, as under the 1987 Act. And no source shows any receiving body admitting people who arrived after 1998.

## What this page can't tell you

Laws and records can't show what it's like to live on land that waits, or how people outside the 1998 list see their future. Oral history and surveys, collected with consent, could begin to answer that.
