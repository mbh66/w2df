The Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials VOC (*Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie*), was a trading company that ruled the Cape from 1652 to 1795 [IC]. It came to the Cape to supply its ships sailing to and from Asia, and stayed to build a colony [IC]. For almost 150 years it shaped what happened in this valley. It traded for the cattle of the [[history/tribes/chainoqua|Chainoqua]] and [[history/tribes/hessequa|Hessequa]], gave out their grazing land as farms, and decided whether missionaries could work among the [[history/tribes/khoekhoe|Khoekhoe]].

## A company that governed

The VOC was founded in the Netherlands in 1602, one of the first companies in the world to sell shares to the public [IC]. The Dutch state gave it a monopoly on Dutch trade with Asia, and the power to govern the places it took [IC]. Over almost two centuries it sent nearly a million Europeans to Asia and carried spices, silk, porcelain, tea, textiles, and enslaved people [IC].

## The Cape station

In 1652 Jan van Riebeeck set up a station at Table Bay for the company, to grow food and supply its ships [IC]. From 1657 the company allowed some of its former servants to farm as "free burghers", selling their crops to the company at fixed prices [IC]. As more colonists took land, the company let them hold large "loan farms" in return for a yearly fee [IC].

Farming for the company depended on forced labour. The company brought enslaved people to the Cape from 1658, from West Africa, Madagascar, Mozambique, India, and South-East Asia [IC]. By the 1790s there were between about 17,000 and 26,000 enslaved people in the colony, depending on the source and the year counted [IC].

## The company and the Khoekhoe

The company needed Khoekhoe cattle, and at first it traded for them [IC]. As colonists took grazing land, the Khoekhoe fought the colony in 1659 to 1660 and in 1673 to 1677 [IC]. In the Overberg, the company traded with the Chainoqua from 1657, and agreed a cattle trade with their leader [[history/people/soeswa|Soeswa]] in 1661 [IC]. From 1662 to 1713 the Chainoqua and Hessequa were its largest suppliers of cattle and sheep [IC]. The Chainoqua leader [[history/people/dorha|Dorha]] served as its go-between from 1679 to 1693, until Governor Simon van der Stel sent him to Robben Island [IC].

The company's expeditions found rich grazing at the Huis River, where Greyton now stands, in 1669 [IC]. By 1727 it was farming timber and cattle at a post between present-day Greyton and Riviersonderend [IC]. In 1745 it set up a magistracy at Swellendam to govern the colonists moving east [IC]. On the northern frontier in the late 1700s, its commandos killed many [[history/tribes/san|San]], and in 1777 it officially backed a policy of wiping them out [IC].

Almost everything written about the Khoekhoe and San of the early Cape comes from the company's own journals and letters [IC]. They are the main source for pages such as Soeswa and Dorha, and they show these peoples through the company's eyes.

## The company and the mission

In 1736 [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] went to Amsterdam to get the company's permission before sailing to the Cape [MS]. When he began to baptise Khoekhoe in 1742, the Cape's Dutch Reformed ministers objected, and on 4 September 1742 the company's Council of Policy banned him from baptising any more [TBV]. He left the Cape in 1744 and was not allowed to return [IC].

The company kept Moravian missionaries out for almost fifty years. When [[history/people/missionaries-of-1792|the missionaries of 1792]] reopened the mission, the company would not let them build a church [IC]. They could build only after the British took the Cape in 1795 [IC]. The company also granted the loan farm Weltevreden, where Greyton now stands, to [[history/people/marthinus-theunissen|Marthinus Theunissen]] in the early 1790s [IC].

## The end of the company

By the late 1700s the company was deep in debt, weakened by corruption, smuggling, and the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War of 1780 to 1784 [IC]. Britain occupied the Cape in 1795 [IC]. The company was dissolved on 31 December 1799, and its possessions passed to the Dutch state [IC]. The Dutch held the Cape again from 1803 to 1806, after which it became a British colony for good [IC].

## What is still open

- What the company's records in the Cape Archives and the Netherlands say about the Chainoqua and Hessequa of this valley, and about Stoffel Koekson.
- The text of the Council of Policy ruling of 4 September 1742.
