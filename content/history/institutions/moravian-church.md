The Moravian Church founded Genadendal, the first mission in southern Africa, and ran it for almost two centuries [IC]. It built the valley's first school, its teachers' college, its printing press, and the church on Church Square, and it held the mission land in trust for residents [IC]. Its congregations in [[moravian-church-genadendal|Genadendal]], [[moravian-church-bereaville|Bereaville]], [[moravian-church-voorstekraal|Voorstekraal]], and [[moravian-church-greyton|Greyton]] are listed in the Register. This page tells the church's history in the valley.

## From Bohemia to the Cape

The church began in 1457 among followers of the Czech reformer Jan Hus [IC]. It was banned in 1620, and its last bishop, [[history/people/jan-amos-comenius|Jan Amos Comenius]], led its members into exile [IC]. Families kept the faith in secret until refugees renewed the church at Herrnhut in Saxony, in 1727 [IC]. From 1732 Herrnhut sent craftsmen overseas as missionaries [IC].

## The mission at Genadendal

- **1737 to 1744.** [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] taught Khoekhoe people near the Sonderend River and at Baviaanskloof, and baptised five people in 1742 [IC]. The Dutch Reformed ministers and the [[history/institutions/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] stopped his work, and he left in 1744 [IC].
- **1744 to 1792.** With no missionary, [[history/people/magdalena|Magdalena]] kept the congregation together [IC].
- **1792.** [[history/people/missionaries-of-1792|Three missionaries]] reopened the mission [IC]. The church counts Genadendal as its first congregation in South Africa [MS].
- **1796 to 1806.** A chapel, a forge, and a mill were built, and the mission was renamed Genadendal [IC].
- **1817 to 1840.** [[history/people/hans-peter-hallbeck|Hans Peter Hallbeck]] led the church's missions in South Africa from Genadendal, and in 1838 founded the teachers' training college [IC].
- **1837 to 1859.** The mission's printing press arrived, and [[history/people/benno-marx|Benno Marx]] founded *De Bode van Genadendal* [IC].
- **1858.** The mission land, now Farm 39, was granted to the church "for the use of and in trust for such persons as may from time to time be lawfully resident at the institution of Genadendal" [MS].
- **1865.** Missionaries started a school at Bereaville [MS].
- **1891 to 1893.** The congregation built the large church that stands on Church Square today [MS].
- **1899.** The church bought the mountain farm Langgezocht to protect Genadendal's water [MS].

The church also grew beyond the valley. From 1828 it worked among Xhosa-speaking people in the Eastern Cape, and in 1867 or 1868 its synod divided the church into two provinces: an Afrikaans-speaking western province and a Xhosa-speaking eastern one [IC].

## Losing the running of Genadendal

In 1909 the Mission Stations and Communal Reserves Act placed mission land under management boards [IC]. Residents could live on the land but not own it, and Forum research describes this as the start of a deep rift between the missionaries and residents [MS]. In 1924 a secular board took over the running of Genadendal from the missionaries [IC]. In 1926 the government closed the teachers' college [IC]. In 1927 about 70 hectares at the heart of Genadendal and Bereaville, the Glebe Lands, were granted to the church, and these sit outside the TRANCRAA process today [IC]. See [[trancraa-process-farm-39|TRANCRAA and Farm 39]].

## One church again

Under apartheid, the division of the church into two provinces along lines of language and race became hard to defend [MS]. In 1991 the two synods resolved to form "one Moravian Church in South Africa", and the united province was established in 1998 [IC]. The church's offices, once at Genadendal, moved to Cape Town [MS].
