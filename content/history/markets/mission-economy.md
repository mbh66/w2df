Genadendal was a village with its own economy, mixing gardens, crafts, trade, and wage work [MS]. It was also a place where money and land ownership were kept apart: residents could live on the land and work it, but not own it [IC]. This page describes how people made a living at the mission. See [[history/markets/money-and-land|Money and Land]].

## Supplies, gardens, and crafts

At Baviaanskloof, [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] exchanged work for supplies [IC]. His students planted gardens, dug a water furrow, and grew wheat, which a neighbouring farmer ground for them [IC]. It was an economy of work and goods, without money.

After [[history/people/missionaries-of-1792|the missionaries of 1792]] reopened the mission, it grew into a craft village. The missionaries were themselves a tailor, a shoemaker, and a knife-maker [MS]. In 1797 they built a forge and a water mill [IC]. The forge made Genadendal knives, which were sold across the Cape [MS]. The mill let the village grind its own grain [MS]. The mission's crafts also included furniture and other goods made by hand [MS]. From 1837 the printing press added printing to the village's trades [IC].

## Wages

Many Khoekhoe in the colony worked on farms. In the early 1800s their wages were paid mostly in kind, above all in sheep. In one district, about four-fifths of wages were paid in kind in 1801, and the share paid in cash grew over the following decade [IC]. Farmers in the Overberg saw the mission as competition for workers, and opposed it for that reason [IC]. Residents of Genadendal also worked for wages on nearby farms, at harvest and at other times [TBV].

## Freed people

After slavery ended in 1838, many freed people came to Genadendal [IC]. Its population grew from 1,446 in 1837 to 2,187 in 1840 [MS]. Newcomers were given a plot to build on, some help to start, and training in a trade, as Khoekhoe families had been [MS].

## Land without title

Residents held plots, gardens, and grazing, but the land was not theirs to own. In 1858 it was granted to the Moravian Church in trust for residents [MS], and in 1909 a law confirmed that residents could live on it but not own it [IC]. Forum research describes this as the start of a deep rift between the missionaries and residents [MS]. Without title deeds, residents could not sell their land on the open market or borrow against it [TBV]. See [[history/markets/land-as-property|Land as Property]].

## Decline

In the second half of the 1800s, factory-made goods arriving by steamship undercut the mission's knives, furniture, and other crafts, and many people left to find work in towns and on farms [TBV]. The printing works lasted until 2003 [MS].

## What is still open

- How residents were paid for mission work, and when money came into everyday use in the village.
- Whether the mission ran a shop, and on what terms.
- What Genadendal knives sold for, and where.
- How many residents worked on farms, and for what wages.
