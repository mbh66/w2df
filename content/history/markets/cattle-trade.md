The cattle trade was the first market in the valley's story. From 1652 the [[history/institutions/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] needed fresh meat for its ships, and bartered for [[history/tribes/khoekhoe|Khoekhoe]] cattle and sheep [IC]. No money changed hands. The company paid in goods: iron, copper, beads, tobacco, and brandy [IC]. For the Khoekhoe of the Overberg, the trade began as an exchange between equals and ended with most of their herds, and their land, in colonial hands [IC]. See [[history/markets/money-and-land|Money and Land]].

## How the trade worked

Khoekhoe wealth was held in cattle, and before the Dutch came Khoekhoe traders already carried goods far into the interior [IC]. The Dutch first met the Chainoqua on 14 November 1657, and regular trading expeditions into the Overberg followed [IC]. In 1661 the Chainoqua leader [[history/people/soeswa|Soeswa]] visited Jan van Riebeeck and agreed a cattle trade [IC]. From 1662 to 1713 the Chainoqua and Hessequa were the company's largest suppliers of cattle and sheep [IC]. From 1679 to 1693 the Chainoqua leader [[history/people/dorha|Dorha]] acted as the company's go-between [IC]. Company expeditions travelled far to trade. In 1689 Ensign Isaq Schrijver's expedition returned with about a thousand head of cattle [IC].

The company kept cattle posts in the Overberg to hold the animals it bartered [IC]. It tried to keep the trade for itself, and colonists who traded with the Khoekhoe on their own account were breaking its rules [TBV].

## What the trade did

The trade took breeding animals out of Khoekhoe herds faster than the herds could grow [TBV]. At the same time, colonists took the grazing land and water that the herds depended on [IC]. Historians describe the Khoekhoe as undermined by "the incessant Dutch demands for their cattle and encroachment on their grazing lands and waterholes" [IC]. After the smallpox epidemic of 1713, the trade faded, and many Khoekhoe who had lost their herds went to work on colonial farms [IC].
