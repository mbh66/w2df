Before 1652, land in this valley was held by groups, and nobody owned it [IC]. Over two hundred years, the [[history/institutions/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] and then British governors turned it into property: land that could be surveyed, registered, rented, sold, and used as security for a loan [IC]. This page follows that change, and shows how the valley ended up with two ways of holding land side by side. See [[history/markets/money-and-land|Money and Land]].

## The first peoples' land

The Khoekhoe had well-developed customary law about land [IC]. Land was held by the community, and seen as a gift from God or from the ancestors [IC]. A group's territory was defined by its water sources. The group was the first user of its springs and rivers, and others needed its permission to graze there [IC]. The [[history/tribes/san|San]] held that no individual owned the land and that everyone had the right to use it [IC].

The company did not recognise these rights as property [IC]. It gave out land as if it were empty, without treaties [IC].

## Freehold, loan farms, and quitrent

From 1657 the company granted colonists surveyed freehold farms in the south-western Cape [IC]. As colonists moved inland with their cattle, the company let them hold large "loan farms" from 1714 [IC]. A loan farm was never surveyed. The holder paid the company a yearly "recognition" fee in money, and had the right to graze, but the land remained the company's [IC].

In 1813 the British governor, [[history/people/john-cradock|Sir John Cradock]], turned loan farms into "perpetual quitrent" farms [IC]. They had to be surveyed, were limited to 3,000 morgen, and could be inherited and sold, in return for a higher yearly rent [IC]. Land became a registered, bounded thing with a price. Many poorer people, including Khoekhoe who had still lived on unsurveyed land, were pushed off [TBV]. From 1843, Crown land was sold as freehold at public auction [IC].

## Weltevreden and Greyton

The farm Weltevreden, where [[villages/greyton|Greyton]] stands, went through each of these stages. Greyton's histories say it had been the land of the Khoekhoe leader [[history/people/stoffel-koekson|Stoffel Koekson]] [TBV]. [[history/people/marthinus-theunissen|Marthinus Theunissen]] held it as a loan farm from 1791, and it was granted to him in 1793 [IC]. It passed to [[history/people/hendrik-cloete|Hendrik Cloete]] in 1795, to [[history/people/john-malcolm-stewart|John Malcolm Stewart]] in 1839, and to [[history/people/herbert-vigne|Herbert Vigne]] in 1846 [IC].

In 1854 Vigne sold the first of Greyton's plots as freehold, each with a title deed, a water right for the leiwater, and grazing rights on the commonage [IC]. Greyton's histories say they were open to buyers of any race [TBV]. Land had become a market. A century later, that market was used against coloured owners, who were forced to sell their plots under the Group Areas Act [IC].

## Genadendal: land held in trust

The mission land at Genadendal took a different path. Mission stations were given land by government "tickets of occupation", usually far smaller than the grazing lands their people had once used [IC]. In 1858 Genadendal's land was granted to the Moravian Church "for the use of and in trust for such persons as may from time to time be lawfully resident at the institution of Genadendal" [MS]. In 1909 the Mission Stations and Communal Reserves Act confirmed that residents could live on the land but not own it [IC].

Residents therefore had homes, gardens, and grazing, but no title deeds [IC]. They could not sell their land on the open market or use it as security for a loan [TBV]. The land has been in the TRANCRAA process since 1998, and has still not been transferred [IC]. See [[trancraa-process-farm-39|TRANCRAA and Farm 39]].
