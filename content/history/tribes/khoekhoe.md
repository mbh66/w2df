The Khoekhoe are the herding peoples of southern Africa, who kept sheep and cattle [IC]. They arrived in the Cape about 2,000 years ago [IC]. In this part of the Overberg, the two main Khoekhoe peoples were the [[history/tribes/chainoqua|Chainoqua]] and the [[history/tribes/hessequa|Hessequa]] [IC]. When [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] opened his school at Baviaanskloof in 1738, his students were Khoekhoe, among them [[history/people/africo|Africo]] and [[history/people/magdalena|Vehettge (Magdalena)]] [IC].

## The name

"Khoekhoe" (also written Khoikhoi) means "people of people", or "real people" [IC]. It was a name of respect the herders used for themselves, and it set them apart from the [[history/tribes/san|San]] hunter-gatherers [IC]. The colonial name "Hottentot" is now considered offensive [IC]. The combined term "Khoisan", for Khoekhoe and San together, was coined by scholars in the 1920s, and some San object to being grouped with the Khoekhoe [IC].

## Way of life

The Khoekhoe moved their herds with the seasons between grazing grounds [IC]. They lived in kraals of *matjieshuise*, houses of reed mats that could be taken down and carried [IC]. Each kraal had a headman, and a camp could hold from 20 to more than 100 houses [IC]. Cattle were the measure of wealth [IC]. Land was held by the group as a whole, and nobody could divide it up for themselves [IC].

The Khoekhoe of the western Cape formed several peoples, each with its own leaders and grazing lands. Among them were the Cochoqua, the Goringhaiqua, the Chainoqua, and the Hessequa [IC].

## Meeting the colony

Portuguese sailors described large Khoekhoe herds at the Cape in 1488 [IC]. In 1510 Khoekhoe warriors defeated a Portuguese force at the Salt River and killed its commander, Francisco de Almeida [IC].

The [[history/institutions/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] set up its station at the Cape in 1652 [IC]. It needed Khoekhoe cattle, and at first it traded for them [IC]. As colonists took grazing land, conflict followed. The Khoekhoe fought the colony in 1659 to 1660 and again in 1673 to 1677 [IC]. In the second war the Chainoqua sided with the Dutch against the Cochoqua [IC].

Smallpox, brought by ships, killed many Khoekhoe in 1713 and again in 1755 [IC]. By the end of the 1700s most Khoekhoe in the western Cape had lost their land and herds, and many worked on colonial farms [IC]. The Caledon Code of 1809 forced them to have a fixed home and carry a pass, until Ordinance 50 of 1828 gave free people of colour legal equality [IC].

Mission stations became one of the few places where Khoekhoe families could live on land of their own [TBV]. More than 1,000 Khoekhoe were living at Genadendal by 1806 [MS]. See [[history/history-of-the-valley|History of the Valley]].

## Today

After 1994, many South Africans of Khoekhoe descent began to reclaim their history, languages, and names [IC]. The Traditional and Khoi-San Leadership Act of 2019 provides for Khoi-San communities and leaders to be recognised by the state for the first time [IC]. Many people of Khoekhoe descent in the Western Cape, including in this valley, belong to coloured communities, and some also identify as Khoi or KhoiSan [TBV].
