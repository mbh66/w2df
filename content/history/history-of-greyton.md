[[villages/greyton|Greyton]] lies at the foot of the Riviersonderend mountains, a few kilometres east of Genadendal. It was laid out in the 1850s on a farm called Weltevreden, on land where Khoekhoe herders had grazed their cattle for centuries [IC]. This page tells Greyton's own story, including [[villages/heuwelkroon|Heuwelkroon]], the part of Greyton to which coloured families were forced to move under apartheid. The story of the whole valley is on [[history/history-of-the-valley|History of the Valley]].

## Before Greyton

The land around Greyton was Khoekhoe grazing land for centuries before the Dutch came [IC]. Greyton's histories say it was home to the [[history/tribes/hessequa|Hessequa]], who kept thousands of cattle and sheep in kraals along the Gobos River, and named the river after an ancestral chief [TBV]. Historians of the early Cape place the [[history/tribes/chainoqua|Chainoqua]], led in the 1600s by [[history/people/soeswa|Soeswa]], on this side of the Breede River, and the Hessequa further east [IC]. Which group grazed the Gobos valley is still open.

In 1669 Dutch travellers found unusually fine pastures at the Huis River, where Greyton now stands [IC]. The Khoekhoe moved their herds with the seasons, and the land looked empty when they were away [IC]. Greyton's histories say that in the late 1600s the Khoekhoe leader [[history/people/stoffel-koekson|Stoffel Koekson]] traded cattle with the Dutch and moved his people to [[villages/boschmanskloof|Boschmanskloof]] [TBV]. Smallpox, brought by ships in 1713, killed many Khoekhoe, and more colonists moved onto the land [IC].

## The farm Weltevreden (1791 to 1854)

The farm on which Greyton was built passed through several hands:

| Year | Owner | What the sources say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1791 | [[history/people/marthinus-theunissen\|Marthinus Theunissen]] | Held it as a loan farm from the VOC [IC]. The farm was granted to him in 1793, and he built a house on it [IC]. Greyton's histories say he left after disputes with his neighbours at Boschmanskloof and Genadendal [TBV]. |
| 1795 | [[history/people/hendrik-cloete\|Hendrik Cloete]] | The owner of Groot Constantia near Cape Town took over the farm [IC]. |
| 1839 | [[history/people/john-malcolm-stewart\|John Malcolm Stewart]] | The first British owner of Weltevreden [IC]. |
| 1846 | [[history/people/herbert-vigne\|Herbert Vigne]] | Bought the farm and later laid out Greyton on it [IC]. |

Herbert Vigne and his older brother Henry (1805 to 1881), from a London family of Huguenot descent, came to the Cape in 1842 [TBV]. Henry farmed Tygerhoek, near present-day Riviersonderend, and the family held it for about 120 years [TBV]. Herbert first settled on a farm at Boschmanskloof, and bought Weltevreden in 1846 [TBV].

## A village for smallholders (1852 to 1900)

Vigne laid out a village on Weltevreden. Sources date its founding to 1852, with the first plots sold in 1854 [IC]. He named it Greyton after Sir George Grey, the Governor of the Cape from 1854 to 1861 [IC]. The surveyor [[history/people/j-g-rietz|J.G. Rietz]] drew the plan, and the village's layout has changed little since [IC].

Greyton was planned as a farming village for smallholders, where each family could grow much of its own food [IC]. The plan gave each owner a long, narrow plot, big enough for a house and a strip of land for crops and a few animals [IC]. Houses were built close to the street, with vegetable gardens and orchards stretching back behind them [MS]. Residents grew onions, pumpkins, apricots, and pears [MS]. Plot owners also shared grazing rights on the commonage around the village [MS].

### The conditions of sale of 1854

[[history/people/helen-robinson|Helen Robinson]]'s 1986 study of Greyton, written at the University of Cape Town, quotes the conditions under which the first plots were sold, from an extract signed by the conveyancer, William Smith of Caledon [TBV]. The extract dates the sale to 27 and 28 December 1854 [TBV]. The Genadendal mission's day-book, also quoted by Robinson, records that 165 plots were sold on 27 November 1854, out of 400 marked out, and that some Genadendal residents bought plots [TBV]. Robinson found in the deeds of sale that at least 120 plots were sold outright in 1854, to buyers from across the Cape, for between £5 and £11 10s each [TBV].

The conditions set out three things:

- **The commonage.** Vigne ceded the rest of his estate, which the conditions give as 1,886 morgen (3,733 acres), as a town commonage, to "remain forever inalienably apportioned" to it [TBV]. Grazing on it was for the owners of plots, people living on plots with the owners' leave, and travellers passing through [TBV]. He kept back 10 morgen in the Magermanskloof, across the river [TBV].
- **Water for the house.** Every inhabitant could take water from the village stream for cooking and household use, without trespassing on private land [TBV].
- **Water for the gardens.** Only the blocks listed in the conditions could use water from the Naauwkloof and the Bosjesmanskloof for irrigation, "in fair and equal proportions, according to the extent of each" [TBV]. Irrigation was a separate right, and Vigne would not be held responsible when the supply changed with the seasons [TBV].

If the extract is correct, the commonage was created at the sale of 1854, and the terms are those above. A copy of the extract from the Deeds Office would confirm the wording.

### The leiwater

The full story is on [[history/leiwater|Greyton's Leiwater]].

Water made the plan work. Greyton's leiwater ("led water") is a network of open furrows that carries water by gravity from the Gobos River to the plots [MS]. It has served the village since its founding in 1854 [MS]. The furrows run along the streets, and each plot with a water right has a set time to open its sluice gate and flood its garden [MS]. Today the water alternates, week by week, between two zones fed from the Bo Lei Dam and the Mid Lei Dam [MS]. Volunteer water bailiffs and street wardens keep the system running [MS]. It is described as one of the last working heritage irrigation systems of its kind in South Africa [MS].

Systems like this share a few rules. Water flows by gravity along the contour, so only plots lying below a furrow can receive it [IC]. Each plot has a turn, or *beurt*, in a fixed cycle, and a water bailiff makes sure everyone gets their turn [IC].

Greyton was one of several villages laid out in this way in the Western Cape in the 1850s, when landowners divided farms into plots and sold them with water rights [IC]. In Montagu, subdivided from 1851, each plot's title deed gave it one hour of irrigation water [IC]. Stanford, laid out in 1855, sold its plots "with reference to the availability of water" [IC]. In many towns these furrows have since stopped working [IC]. In Montagu, the old water rights written into title deeds were replaced by the National Water Act of 1998 [IC]. How that Act affects Greyton's leiwater rights has not yet been checked [TBV]. South African History Online says the plots, with title deeds, water rights, and grazing rights, were sold to buyers of any race, nationality, or religion, and calls Greyton the only town in the Cape where such land was open to anyone [TBV]. Robinson's reading of the Deeds Office records points the same way: she found that land in Greyton was sold and rented "without regard for colour" from 1854 until the Group Areas Act was applied in 1969 [TBV]. Names of British and of other origins appear side by side on the deeds of sale of 1856 and the early 1860s [TBV].

Within fifty years residents had built houses, businesses, and smallholdings, opened a school, and built two churches [TBV]. The [[history/people/de-villiers-family|De Villiers family]] built a trading store in 1860 [TBV]. The Post House, built in 1860 as a trading post and lodging for travellers and later Greyton's post office, is one of the oldest buildings in the village and a Provincial Heritage Site [IC]. By 1891 Greyton had 276 residents [IC]. Robinson gives the 1891 census as 1,186 people, and notes that the figure covers the area around the village, because Greyton was not counted on its own until it became a municipality in 1910 [TBV]. Before then the village was run by three commissioners, named as Dreyer, Mays, and Schultz, under regulations proclaimed in 1862 and 1865 [TBV].

From 1865 to 1880, Greyton and McGregor tried to build a road over the mountains between them, through the Boesmanskloof. They raised £700, but the money ran out [TBV]. Robinson dates the first tender, for a footpath through the Boesmanskloof to Lady Grey (now McGregor), to 1863, when Vigne was allowed £800 for it [TBV].

### Schools, churches, and the canteen

Robinson traces the first schools and churches through church records and the memories of older residents [TBV].

- **Anglican.** From 1864 Rev. Middleton and Mr Wilscher of the Anglican church in Caledon came by horse and cart to hold services in Greyton [TBV]. In about 1866 or 1867 Wilscher opened the village's first school, open to all children, which people called the "Engelse skool" [TBV]. By 1900 it had at least 100 children, and older residents classified coloured remembered learning there with white children [TBV]. Vigne set aside a plot for a church, which was built in 1904 [TBV].
- **Moravian.** From 1864, at Vigne's invitation, the Genadendal missionaries held services in the English school for Moravian members in Greyton and Boschmanskloof [TBV]. By 1893 they had taken over a small school building, and by 1900 about 80 children attended [TBV].
- **Dutch Reformed.** Dutch-speaking families held services in homes or in the open, led by a minister from Caledon [TBV]. A school for white children only began in the front room of the [[history/people/de-villiers-family|De Villiers family]]'s home, with Caterina Mays as teacher [TBV]. Under Miss von Solms, head from 1888 to 1905, it grew from 19 to 103 pupils [TBV].

Robinson writes that before the Group Areas Act, the clearest division in Greyton's society ran along church lines [TBV]. The Dutch Reformed church served white families, the Moravian church mainly coloured families, and the Anglican church both [TBV].

In 1860 the liquor law changed. Despite a petition from the missionaries and 500 Genadendal residents, a "canteen" was licensed in Greyton, about two miles from Genadendal, and Genadendal residents began to fetch wine there [TBV]. Robinson quotes the Genadendal day-book on this [TBV].

In the 1860s Vigne married [[history/people/elizabeth-vigne|Elizabeth Belshaw]], whose family home still stands in the village [MS]. Vigne died in 1895 [IC]. Robinson writes that he sold plots in batches of twenty or thirty until his death, and that his heirs sold the rest until about 1910 [TBV]. Parts of the building that is now Greyton Lodge date from 1882, and it once held the village's police cells [MS]. Greyton's histories say the village then went through hard economic times [TBV].

## The 1900s

- **1910.** Greyton became a municipality [IC].
- **1911.** The census counted 753 people in the village: 257 white and 496 coloured [TBV]. Census figures gathered by Robinson show the village growing to 1,094 people in 1946, then falling to 707 by 1980 [TBV].
- **1904.** St Andrew's Anglican Church was built [MS].
- **1918.** The Dutch Reformed congregation of Greyton (NG Gemeente) was founded on 7 May 1918 [MS]. Its present church was built in 1964 [MS].
- **1920s and 1930s.** Jan de Villiers opened a shop in his home in the 1920s [MS]. The grandfather of the Osman family came to Greyton in 1920, and A.G. Osman and Sons moved to its present building in 1934 [MS]. A building that was first a Dutch Reformed church is now the Moravian church in Greyton [IC].
- **1927 to 1934.** After an outbreak of typhoid in 1927, the first piped water scheme was finished in 1933 [TBV]. A bridge replaced the ferry over the Riviersonderend in 1934 [TBV]. The first Anglican services were held in a flat-roofed building that later became the Moravian school and is now the town library [TBV].
- **1924 to 1941.** A new attempt at the Boesmanskloof road began with a survey in 1924. Work resumed in 1936 as a poverty relief scheme, and stopped in 1940 or 1941 when the Second World War took the men and money [TBV]. The road was never finished [IC]. Robinson gives a different account: a survey by J. de Villiers in 1927, work from the Robertson side from 1938 with £25,000, first by convicts and then by Italian prisoners of war, and an end in 1941 at the place called Die Galg [TBV]. She dates the proclamation of the footpath as a hiking trail to 1977 [TBV]. Its unfinished route is now the 14-kilometre Boesmanskloof hiking trail to McGregor, known as the "road to nowhere" [IC].

## The removals to Heuwelkroon

The Group Areas Act of 1950 divided South Africa's towns by race [IC]. In Greyton, coloured families, many of whom owned their own plots and small farms, were forced to sell and leave [IC]. Most were moved to Heuwelkroon, a new township on a hill on the edge of the village [IC]. A 2022 heritage study says some went to Genadendal [TBV]. Robinson says others bought land at Boschmanskloof, and some moved to Cape Town or other towns [TBV].

Sources differ on the dates. Some Greyton histories say the 1950s [TBV]. An essay in the arts journal *Herri* dates the removals from 1968 to 1974, and the 2022 heritage study says Heuwelkroon was built in 1970 [TBV]. Robinson's study, written in the 1980s from records and interviews, dates the proclamation to 1968 or 1969 and most removals to 1969 to 1971 [TBV]. The three sources agree that the removals took place from the late 1960s to the mid-1970s [IC].

### What happened, and when

The fullest account is Helen Robinson's 1986 study, which draws on Deeds Office records, newspaper reports, the papers of Dr Oscar Wollheim, and interviews with residents in 1984 and 1985 [TBV]. Unless another source is named, the facts in this part of the page come from it.

- **1964 to 1965.** A committee of the Group Areas Board visited the district [TBV]. Coloured ratepayers protested, and said they wished to stay on their land, which for many was their living [TBV]. The Coloured Ratepayers Association sent a petition signed by 22 ratepayers [TBV]. Dr Oscar Wollheim, the Coloured Persons' Representative on the Provincial Council, told the *Cape Argus* that if any group had the right to remain in the town, history showed it was the coloured group [TBV].
- **1967.** An advertisement in the Caledon *Venster* on 17 March said that municipal land was being set aside for relocation [TBV]. The Director of Local Government told Dr Wollheim that the application came from the Greyton Municipality [TBV]. Dr Wollheim replied that the land was unfit for homes or small farming, and that houses stood empty in Greyton at the time [TBV].
- **1968 to 1969.** The main part of Greyton was declared a white group area in the *Government Gazette* [TBV]. Robinson dates the newspaper report to August 1968 and the official announcement to 1969 [TBV].
- **1969 to 1971.** Officials served notices to leave, and families who rented were moved first [TBV]. Owners were pressed to sell, and property speculators and agents arrived [TBV]. One widow told Robinson she was told to hand her key in at the police station; she sold her house and plot for R1,250 [TBV].
- **By 1975.** All 100 houses in Heuwelkroon were occupied [TBV]. Eight families classified coloured still lived on their own land in the village [TBV].
- **1977 to 1978.** The town clerk wrote to the eight families that they must leave [TBV]. They refused, and some white residents supported them [TBV]. The *Sunday Times* and the *Cape Times* reported their case [TBV]. Officials of the Group Areas Board and the Department of Community Development visited, and the families were told they could stay and farm, for the owner's lifetime only [TBV].
- **1979 to 1980.** Three of those owners died. Their wives and sons were not allowed to inherit, and the properties were sold to white buyers [TBV].

### How many were affected

Newspapers at the time gave a figure of about 30 owners [TBV]. Robinson's search of the Deeds Office records found about 55 families classified coloured who owned property in Greyton in 1969 and 1970, holding more than 80 plots [TBV]. That was almost half of all property owners in the village [TBV]. At least 50 of these properties were sold from 1969 to 1974, and she traced 66 that passed to white owners from 1969 to 1986 [TBV]. At least fifteen more families rented land and were moved too [TBV].

Robinson's thesis lists the owners of 1969 by name and plot number [TBV]. This page does not repeat the names. Families who would like their story told may contact the moderators.

### Heuwelkroon in the 1980s

Robinson describes Heuwelkroon in 1985 and 1986:

- The houses were sub-economic houses rented from the municipality for R10 to R20 a month, depending on the household's income [TBV].
- From 1970 water came only from taps in the street, and there was no water-borne sanitation [TBV]. Work on a tap in each yard began in 1986 [TBV].
- Electricity reached only the school and a few privately owned houses [TBV].
- The amenities on the township's plan had not been built, and the land between the houses and the main road was open and stony [TBV].
- A new school opened in 1980, and replaced the Anglican church school and the Moravian mission school [TBV]. It may be the school that is now [[greyton-primary|Greyton Primary School]] [TBV].
- Of 50 households classified coloured in her sample, 26 per cent of household heads worked in Greyton, 44 per cent worked outside it, 10 per cent were unemployed, and 20 per cent were retired [TBV].

Older residents told Robinson of a "golden age" before the removals, when white and coloured neighbours farmed, worked, and visited side by side [TBV]. Robinson notes that such memories are selective, and that white "colour consciousness" was visible from the 1930s [TBV]. She also records that marriages and families across the colour line were common in the village's early years [TBV]. In the graveyard, white and coloured residents were first buried close together; over the years the graves drifted apart, so that white residents now lie in the newer section higher up the hill, and coloured residents in the older, lower section [TBV].

The *Herri* essay says that before the removals, Greyton's residents "lived together irrespective of 'race'", and that afterwards "a community of subsistence farmers who owned their own farms were overnight reduced to having to work as gardeners and domestics for white people" [MS].

In the mid-1990s, a transitional council replaced Greyton's white municipal council and the Heuwelkroon Management Committee [IC].

## Mountain and commonage

On 13 May 1977 the [[economic/farmers-land-water/greyton-nature-reserve|Greyton Nature Reserve]] was proclaimed on the mountain commonage [IC]. It was established by Professor [[history/people/brian-rycroft|Brian Rycroft]], then Director of Kirstenbosch [IC]. It covers about 2,200 hectares, up to the peaks of Uitkyk, Wa en Osse, and Perdekop [IC]. The [[greyton-conservation-society|Greyton Conservation Society]] has helped look after it since 1979 [MS].

The reserve protects Greyton Shale Fynbos, a plant community named after the village, which was listed as Critically Endangered in 2022 [IC]. In December 2019 a fire burned about 5,950 hectares on the mountain slopes above Greyton [IC].

Greyton's histories say Vigne left the land around the village to the plot owners as a commonage [TBV]. His will or deed of gift has not been found [TBV]. The conditions of sale of 1854, quoted by Robinson, give terms for the commonage (see *The conditions of sale of 1854* above) [TBV]. Robinson writes that when the village boundaries were extended onto the commonage for the white group area, on 25 June 1973 and 25 June 1976, the old servitudes on the use of the commonage and on water rights in the first title deeds were cancelled [TBV]. By 1986 the municipality had sold 60 plots in these extensions [TBV]. The commonage passed to Theewaterskloof Municipality in 2000 [TBV].

## Recent years

- **December 2000.** Greyton became part of the new Theewaterskloof Municipality [IC].
- **2011.** A group of residents started Greyton Transition Town, to respond to rising fuel and food costs [MS].
- **2019.** Residents of [[villages/madiba-park|Madiba Park]], on Farm 39 land next to Heuwelkroon, protested over housing and services [IC]. See [[madiba-park-land-and-services|Madiba Park: Land Status and Basic Services]].
- **2025.** The province granted environmental authorisation for housing on Erf 595 in Heuwelkroon [IC].

## What is still open

- Which Khoekhoe group grazed the Gobos valley, and what they called it.
- Who dug the first leiwater furrows and dams, and how the water rights were written into the first title deeds.
- How the National Water Act of 1998 affects the leiwater rights attached to Greyton's plots.
- Whether the first plots were really sold to buyers of any race. Robinson's reading of the deeds says they were; the first title deeds would confirm it.
- Whether the first plots were sold on 27 November or on 27 and 28 December 1854. The Genadendal day-book and the conditions of sale give different dates.
- Whether the conditions of sale of 1854, as Robinson quotes them, set the full terms of the commonage, and what the cancellation of its servitudes in 1973 and 1976 means for plot owners today.
- Which families were moved, and how their stories should be told. Robinson's thesis lists the owners of 1969 by name.
- Where Greyton's first school buildings stood, and which of them survive.
- The stories of Heuwelkroon, told by the families who were moved there.

Used for the conditions of sale of 1854 and the Genadendal day-book entries, the first schools and churches, the canteen, the census figures, the Boesmanskloof road, the Group Areas removals from 1964 to 1980, the number of owners affected, Heuwelkroon in the 1980s, and the extensions onto the commonage.
- Williams, R. ["Hoekom soek ons nog altyd *Kos Klere en Blyplek* in die nuwe dispensasie?"](https://herri.org.za/2/rafieka-williams/). *Herri*, issue 2 (no date given). A personal essay built around a reggae song by Country Conquerors, by a Capetonian writer who came to Greyton in May 2017 and spoke with residents. *Herri* is an online arts and culture journal published by Stephanus Muller for the Africa Open Institute and edited by Aryan Kaganof (see [Review: herri is a rare new arts and culture journal from South Africa](https://www.modernghana.com/amp/news/1003294/review-herri-is-a-rare-new-arts-and-culture-journ.html)). Used here for the dates of the removals, 1968 to 1974, and their effects.
- ACO Associates for Ohana Environmental Consulting (2022). [Heritage Impact Assessment, Erf 595, Greyton](https://ohanaenviro.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/HIA-Greyton-Heritage.pdf). The founding, the 1891 population, removals to Heuwelkroon and Genadendal, and Heuwelkroon built in 1970.
