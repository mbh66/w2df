Ward 2 lies in the valley of the Sonderend River, below the Riviersonderend mountains. People have lived here for a very long time. This page tells the valley's story in the order it happened, from the first stone tools to the first meeting of the Ward 2 Development Forum in 2026. It covers [[villages/genadendal|Genadendal]], [[villages/greyton|Greyton]], and the smaller settlements around them. A full list of dates is at the end of the page.

## The first people

Stone tools found near Riviersonderend show that people lived in this area more than 200,000 years ago [IC]. Archaeologists have found tools from the Early, Middle, and Late Stone Age on the northern, eastern, and southern sides of the Riviersonderend mountain [IC]. No full archaeological survey of the mountains has been done, so much is still to be found [IC].

The first people known by name are the [[history/tribes/soaqua|Soaqua]], the [[history/tribes/san|San]] hunter-gatherers of this part of the Cape, who lived by hunting and gathering [IC]. Their rock paintings survive in the mountains, mostly on the drier northern side [IC]. Traces of the Soaqua have been found in the mountains north of Genadendal [IC]. Fourteen cave sites in the mountains are on a heritage list, but they have not been formally surveyed or dated [TBV].

About 2,000 years ago, [[history/tribes/khoekhoe|Khoekhoe]] herders arrived with sheep and cattle [IC]. The Soaqua moved higher into the mountains, where they lived on plants and small animals such as tortoises and dassies [IC].

By the 1600s the Overberg was home to two main Khoekhoe groups, the [[history/tribes/chainoqua|Chainoqua]] and the [[history/tribes/hessequa|Hessequa]] [IC]. Chainoqua land stretched from the Hottentots Holland mountains towards Cape Agulhas and inland to the Riviersonderend mountains [IC]. Some of their kraals stood near where Genadendal is today [IC]. Each kraal had a headman, and a camp could hold from 20 to more than 100 *matjieshuise* (reed-mat houses) [IC]. Cattle were the measure of wealth [IC]. Land was held by the group as a whole, and nobody could divide it up for themselves [IC].

## Contact with the Dutch (1652 to 1737)

The [[history/institutions/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] (VOC) set up a station at the Cape in 1652 [IC]. Its people first met the Chainoqua on 14 November 1657 [IC]. In 1661 the Chainoqua leader [[history/people/soeswa|Soeswa]] visited Jan van Riebeeck and agreed to trade cattle [IC]. For about fifty years after that, the Chainoqua and Hessequa were the main suppliers of cattle to the Cape [IC]. From 1679 to 1693 the Chainoqua clan leader [[history/people/dorha|Dorha]], whom the Dutch called Klaas, acted as the VOC's go-between with the Overberg Khoekhoe [IC].

In 1669 Dutch travellers found rich grazing at the Huis River, where Greyton stands today [IC]. The Khoekhoe moved their herds with the seasons, so the land looked empty when they were away [IC]. Settlers took this as a sign that the land was free to take [IC]. Greyton sources say that in the late 1600s the Hessequa leader [[history/people/stoffel-koekson|Stoffel Koekson]] moved his people to [[villages/boschmanskloof|Boschmanskloof]], and that the foundations of their houses lie under the old houses there [TBV].

In 1713 smallpox, brought by passing ships, killed many Khoekhoe [IC]. More settlers moved onto the land they left behind [IC]. By 1727 the VOC was farming timber and cattle between present-day Greyton and Riviersonderend [IC]. Independent Khoekhoe kraals were still recorded near Genadendal as late as 1803 [IC].

## Where the missionaries came from

The mission at Genadendal grew out of a church with a long history in central Europe.

- In 1415 the Czech reformer Jan Hus was burned for heresy [IC]. People who followed his teaching founded the Unitas Fratrum (Unity of the Brethren) in 1457 [IC]. In English this church is known as the Moravian Church.
- In 1620 the Protestant side lost the Battle of White Mountain, and the church was banned in Bohemia and Moravia [IC].
- [[history/people/jan-amos-comenius|Jan Amos Comenius]] (1592 to 1670) was a bishop of the church and its best-known thinker [IC]. He is often called the father of modern education [IC]. He argued that every child, girl or boy, rich or poor, should go to school, and that children learn best by seeing and doing, in their own language [IC]. His *Orbis Pictus* (1658) was the first picture book made for teaching children [IC].
- After Comenius died, small groups of families kept the faith in secret for about fifty years. The church calls them the "Hidden Seed" [IC].
- In 1722 Moravian refugees built the village of Herrnhut in Saxony, on the estate of Count Zinzendorf [IC]. The church was renewed there on 13 August 1727 [IC].
- From 1732 Herrnhut sent missionaries overseas [IC]. They were craftsmen who earned their own living and lived among the people they served [IC].

[[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] came from this community. He was born in Moravia in 1709 and had spent years in prison and forced labour in Bohemia for preaching [IC].

Schmidt's school at Baviaanskloof matches many of Comenius's ideas. It was open to all who came, it taught reading in the local language, and it taught through work in the gardens and fields [MS]. Some researchers think Schmidt carried the Comenius tradition with him from Herrnhut, but this has not been shown [TBV]. A 1961 study by Jerome Clauser, on Comenius and Moravian schools, may settle the question. It has not yet been read for this page.

## Georg Schmidt at Baviaanskloof (1737 to 1744)

Schmidt landed at the Cape on 9 July 1737 [IC]. Later that year he began teaching Khoekhoe people in Dutch, first at a kraal where [[history/people/africo|Africo]] was the clan leader [MS]. On 23 April 1738 he settled at Baviaanskloof ("baboon ravine"), where Genadendal stands [IC]. By the end of 1738, 28 people were living and learning at the station [IC].

Schmidt taught reading and writing under a pear tree [IC]. He dug a water furrow and planted vegetable gardens, and people worked in exchange for supplies [IC]. In 1739 he built a hartebeeshuis (a house of reeds and clay), a sheep kraal, and a threshing floor [IC]. His students grew wheat, but they had no mill, so a neighbouring farmer ground it for them [IC].

In 1742 Schmidt received permission to baptise. He baptised five people: [[history/people/kupido-and-willem|Willem and Kupido]], Africo, [[history/people/christina|Christina]], and Vehettge Tikkuie, who took the name [[history/people/magdalena|Magdalena]] [MS]. Vehettge's husband, [[history/people/janneke-tikkuie|Janneke Tikkuie]], who hunted and fetched supplies for the mission, was not baptised with her [MS]. Local Dutch Reformed ministers objected that he had no right to baptise [IC]. Farmers were also unhappy to lose cheap Khoekhoe labour [IC]. Schmidt left the Cape in 1744 and was never allowed to return [IC]. He died at Niesky, in Germany, in 1785 [IC].

## Magdalena and the pear tree (1744 to 1792)

For 48 years there was no missionary at Baviaanskloof [IC]. Magdalena kept the small congregation together [IC]. She gathered people under the pear tree in Schmidt's garden to pray, and read to them from the Dutch New Testament Schmidt had given her [IC]. She kept it wrapped in sheepskin [MS].

When new missionaries arrived in 1792, they found Magdalena, old and nearly blind, still holding on to that New Testament [MS]. She died on 3 January 1800 [IC]. The historians Henry Bredekamp and Christina Landman have written her story [IC].

## The mission grows (1792 to 1838)

[[history/people/missionaries-of-1792|Three Moravian missionaries]] arrived at Baviaanskloof on 24 December 1792 [IC]. Hendrik Marsveld was a tailor, Daniel Schwinn a shoemaker, and Johann Christian Kühnel a knife-maker [MS]. Eight kraals of Khoekhoe moved to the kloof, and formal lessons began on 4 March 1793 with 25 adults [MS]. The school has run ever since. It is now [[lr-schmidt-primary|L.R. Schmidt Primary School]] [MS].

The VOC would not let the missionaries build a church. They could build only after the British took the Cape in 1795 [IC]. A chapel was consecrated in 1796 [IC]. In March 1797 the community built a forge, and a water mill at about the same time [IC]. The forge made Genadendal knives, which were sold across the Cape [MS]. A larger church was finished by 1800 [IC].

In 1806 Governor Janssens visited and renamed the mission Genadendal, "valley of grace" [IC]. More than 1,000 Khoekhoe were living there by then [MS].

The mission became a place of learning and trades. In 1819 and 1820 the missionary J.D. Beinbach and members of the congregation built a bridge over the Sonderend River. It is now a Provincial Heritage Site [IC]. In 1823 Genadendal opened a lending library, with books in English, Dutch, and German and a reading room [IC]. A printing press arrived in 1837, and it is now in the [[genadendal-mission-museum|Genadendal Mission Museum]] [IC]. The mission buildings around Church Square (die Werf) are a Provincial Heritage Site [IC].

Laws outside the mission shaped who came to it. The Caledon Code of 1809 forced Khoekhoe people to have a fixed home and to carry a pass [IC]. Mission stations became one of the few places where they could live without working for a farmer [TBV]. Ordinance 50 of 1828 gave free people of colour legal equality and ended the pass laws [IC].

## Emancipation and the busy years (1834 to 1860s)

Slavery at the Cape ended in law on 1 December 1834. People who had been enslaved then had to work four more years as "apprentices" [IC]. They became fully free on 1 December 1838 [IC]. In the countryside, mission stations were among the few places where freed people could find land and a home, and many came to Genadendal [IC]. Mission records show its population growing from 1,446 in 1837 to 2,187 in 1840 [MS]. Newcomers were given a plot to build on and were taught a trade, as Khoekhoe families had been [MS].

In 1838 South Africa's first teachers' training college opened at Genadendal [IC]. It was founded by [[history/people/hans-peter-hallbeck|Hans Peter Hallbeck]], who led the Moravian missions in South Africa from Genadendal from 1817 to 1840 [MS]. Mission sources give the date as 12 September 1838 [MS]. It trained teachers for almost ninety years [IC]. Music was part of its teaching, and brass playing began there. By Easter 1856 Genadendal had a brass band, which still plays today [MS]. See [[history/music-of-the-valley|Music of the Valley]].

From 1859 the mission press printed *De Bode van Genadendal*, a monthly journal founded by the missionary [[history/people/benno-marx|Benno Marx]] [IC]. It was written in the Dutch spoken at Genadendal, one of the roots of written Afrikaans, and it came out 17 years before *Die Afrikaanse Patriot* (1876) [IC]. From 1914 it appeared as *Die Huisvriend* [TBV].

In 1858 the mission land was granted to the Moravian Church "for the use of and in trust for such persons as may from time to time be lawfully resident at the institution of Genadendal" [MS]. This is the land now called Farm 39 [MS]. The trust for residents lies at the heart of the land questions the valley still faces. See [[trancraa-process-farm-39|TRANCRAA and Farm 39: The Land Transfer Process]].

The mission's work reached nearby settlements. Moravian missionaries started a school at [[villages/bereaville|Bereaville]] in 1865, which is now [[berea-primary|Berea Primary School]] [MS]. Bereaville and [[villages/voorstekraal|Voorstekraal]] each have a Moravian congregation [MS]. The early history of both villages has still to be written.

## Greyton is founded (1793 to 1910)

In 1793 the land around the Gobos River, once Hessequa grazing land, was granted to a young Dutchman, [[history/people/marthinus-theunissen|Marthinus Theunissen]], who farmed it as Weltevreden [IC]. [[history/people/hendrik-cloete|Hendrik Cloete]] of Groot Constantia took it over in 1795 [IC]. It passed in 1839 to [[history/people/john-malcolm-stewart|John Malcolm Stewart]], its first British owner [IC]. In 1846 an Englishman, [[history/people/herbert-vigne|Herbert Vigne]], bought Weltevreden [IC]. Some sources give 1845.

Vigne laid out a village on the farm. Sources date its founding to 1852, with the first plots sold in 1854 [IC]. He named it Greyton after Sir George Grey, the Governor of the Cape [IC]. The surveyor [[history/people/j-g-rietz|J.G. Rietz]] drew the town plan [IC]. It was planned as a farming village for smallholders: each long, narrow plot had room for a house, crops, and a few animals [IC]. Water reached the plots by gravity through open furrows from the Gobos River, the leiwater system that still works today [MS]. The plots came with title deeds, water rights, and grazing rights, and were sold to buyers of any race [TBV]. Greyton sources say Vigne left the land around the village to the plot owners as a commonage, but his will or deed of gift has not been found [TBV]. The Post House, built in 1860, is one of Greyton's oldest buildings and a Provincial Heritage Site [IC]. Greyton histories say the [[history/people/de-villiers-family|De Villiers family]] built a trading store in the same year [TBV]. In the 1860s Vigne married [[history/people/elizabeth-vigne|Elizabeth Belshaw]] [IC]. By 1891 Greyton had 276 residents [IC]. Greyton became a municipality in 1910 [IC]. Greyton's fuller story is on [[history/history-of-greyton|History of Greyton]].

## Hard years: land, work, and law (1860s to 1950)

In the second half of the 1800s, cheap factory-made goods reached the Cape by steamship. They undercut the knives, furniture, and other goods made by hand at Genadendal, and many people left to find work in towns and on farms [TBV].

Between 1891 and 1893 the congregation built the large church that stands on Church Square today [MS]. In 1899 the Moravian Church bought the mountain farm Langgezocht, about 1,881 hectares, to protect the water sources of Farm 39 [MS].

In 1909 the Mission Stations and Communal Reserves Act placed mission land under management boards. Residents kept the right to live on the land, but they could not own it [IC]. Forum research describes this as the start of a deep rift between the missionaries and residents [MS]. In 1924 a secular board of management took over from the missionaries [IC].

In 1926 the government closed the teachers' college [IC]. It argued that coloured people did not need higher education because they were suited to work as labourers on farms [IC]. In 1927 about 70 hectares at the heart of Genadendal and Bereaville, known as the Glebe Lands, were granted to the Moravian Church [IC].

## Apartheid (1950 to 1994)

The Group Areas Act of 1950 divided land by race [IC]. In Greyton, coloured families were forced to sell their homes and farmland and move to [[villages/heuwelkroon|Heuwelkroon]], on the edge of the village [IC]. Sources differ on the dates. Some Greyton histories say the 1950s. An essay in the journal *Herri* dates the removals from 1968 to 1974, and a 2022 heritage study says Heuwelkroon was built in 1970 [TBV]. See [[history/history-of-greyton|History of Greyton]].

On 13 May 1977 the Greyton Nature Reserve, established by [[history/people/brian-rycroft|Brian Rycroft]], was proclaimed on the mountain commonage [IC]. It covers about 2,200 hectares [IC].

In 1987 the Rural Areas Act 9 of 1987 took over the former mission areas, and Genadendal was added to it in 1989 [IC]. On 8 March 1991 the Mission Museum's collection was declared a National Cultural Treasure [MS].

## Democracy and the land question (1994 to 2011)

South Africa held its first democratic election on 27 April 1994 [IC]. In 1995 President Nelson Mandela renamed his official Cape Town residence, then called Westbrooke, Genadendal [IC]. Mission sources say the name came from a letter by the museum curator, Dr Isaac Balie [MS]. Mandela visited Genadendal on 10 October 1995 [IC]. He later described it as "a place of sanctuary for more than a thousand slaves when slavery was abolished in 1838" [MS].

In the mid-1990s a transitional council replaced Greyton's white municipal council and the Heuwelkroon management committee [IC]. In December 2000 the valley became part of the new Theewaterskloof Municipality [IC].

TRANCRAA, the law meant to hand Farm 39 to an entity its residents help choose, came into force on 2 November 1998 [IC]. The municipality began the process in 2002 [IC]. Residents elected the [[civic/governance/genadendal-transformasie-komitee|Transformasie Komitee]] in November 2004 [IC]. A settlement between the Komitee, the municipality, and the national Minister became a High Court order on 22 October 2008, and its terms were never carried out [IC]. The transitional period for Farm 39 ended in January 2011 without a transfer, and the land stayed with the Minister [IC]. The full story, with each party's position in its own words, is on [[trancraa-process-farm-39|TRANCRAA and Farm 39: The Land Transfer Process]].

The Genadendal printing works closed in 2003, after more than 170 years [MS].

## Recent years (2011 to 2026)

- In 2011 a group of Greyton residents started Greyton Transition Town, in response to rising fuel and food costs [MS].
- In 2019 residents of [[villages/madiba-park|Madiba Park]], on Farm 39 land, protested over housing and services [IC]. See [[madiba-park-land-and-services|Madiba Park: Land Status and Basic Services]].
- The national COVID-19 lockdown began on 27 March 2020 [IC]. Small-scale and commercial farmers in the valley set up a community feeding scheme [MS].
- In 2024 Ranyaka held community sessions across the valley. Between 50 and 89 people came to each [MS].
- In 2025 Theewaterskloof Municipality was placed under a Financial Recovery Plan [IC].
- In July 2026 the Valley of Grace Nature Realm, covering about 10,000 hectares of the valley, went live on the Guardians of Earth platform [MS].
- On 16 September 2026 the [[civic/governance/ward-2-development-forum|Ward 2 Development Forum (W2DF)]] met for the first time, at Hester Dorothea in Genadendal [MS]. See the [[civic/governance/minutes/2026-09-16-inaugural-meeting|minutes of the inaugural meeting]].

## Looking ahead

April 2028 marks 290 years since Schmidt settled at Baviaanskloof. On 23 April 2038 Genadendal turns 300. The Forum has set its long-term goal against that date. See [[long-term-goal|Our Long-Term Goal]].

## Where the sources disagree

- **When Genadendal was founded.** Schmidt reached the Cape in 1737 and began teaching nearby that year. He settled at Baviaanskloof on 23 April 1738 [IC]. The provincial heritage register gives 1737 [IC]. The museum, the mission, and the 2038 tercentenary use 1738 [MS].
- **When Schmidt left.** Most sources say 1744. Some say 1743 [IC].
- **The first church.** A chapel was consecrated in 1796 [IC], and a larger church was finished by 1800 [IC]. Magdalena died on 3 January 1800, shortly before the new church was consecrated [MS].
- **Greyton's founding.** Vigne bought Weltevreden in 1845 or 1846. The village is dated to 1852 or 1854, depending on whether the source counts the plan or the first sale of plots [IC].
- **The removals to Heuwelkroon.** Some Greyton histories give the 1950s. The *Herri* essay gives 1968 to 1974, and the 2022 heritage study says Heuwelkroon was built in 1970. The later two agree [TBV].
- **Greyton Transition Town.** Its own website says it started in mid-2011. Forum research notes give 2012 [MS].
- **The size of Farm 39.** Records give between about 4,515 and 4,800 hectares. See [[trancraa-process-farm-39|TRANCRAA and Farm 39]].

## Help fill the gaps

Much of the valley's history is written from mission records and Greyton sources. Some parts are missing:

- The history of the Khoekhoe and San in the valley, as their descendants tell it, and the old names of places.
- How Bereaville, Voorstekraal, Boschmanskloof, and Madiba Park began.
- The removals from Greyton to Heuwelkroon: when they happened, who was moved, and what families remember.
- Photographs, letters, and documents held by families, congregations, and schools.

## Reading the history through TIME

Some of the Forum's research reads the valley's past with a framework called TIME. It builds on the work of the American researcher David Ronfeldt, who called his version TIMN. TIME asks one question of each period: how did people organise themselves to share work, land, and what they produced? It names four forms. Each new form is added to the ones before it, and the older forms carry on alongside it.

| Form | How people organise | In this valley |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **T: Tribes** | Kinship and belonging. What you give and get depends on your family and clan. | The Soaqua and Khoekhoe, before 1738 |
| **I: Institutions** | Written rules, leaders with authority, and fixed settlements. | The mission, from 1738 |
| **M: Markets** | Buying and selling at a price. | From 1806, Genadendal's crafts were sold across the Cape, and makers lived from their work. From about the 1860s, the market worked more for outside owners than for the valley's makers. |
| **E: Emergent** | Sharing, commons, and care for the land, linked to similar places around the world. | Starting in 2020, with the community response to COVID-19 |

This is one way of reading the history. Residents may read the same events differently. The Forum's research uses it to ask what each period can teach the next one. See [[long-term-goal|Our Long-Term Goal]].
