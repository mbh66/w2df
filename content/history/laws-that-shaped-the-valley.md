For more than three hundred years, laws made far from the valley decided who could graze its land, who could live on it, who had to work for whom, and which children went to school. This page brings those laws together in one place. It includes the laws that reached the valley directly, and the national racial laws that shaped the whole country and so shaped the valley too. It also includes the orders the missionaries wrote down in their diaries between 1792 and 1805, because those orders show how the law actually arrived: spoken at the church door by the keeper of the company post, or carried up the kloof by a field cornet. A few rows are not laws at all. They are market signals large enough to change what the law and the valley may do next, and they are marked as such.

Each law is read through the TIME model used on [[history/the-valley-through-time|The Valley Through TIME]]: which of the forms of organisation it built up, which it held back, and whether it carries any early sign of the Emergent form.

> [!info] What the letters in square brackets mean
> Facts on this page are followed by a tag that shows where they come from.
>
> - **[IC] Independently Corroborated.** Confirmed by at least two sources that do not depend on each other, or by an official record such as a law, a court order, or a heritage register.
> - **[MS] Mission-Sourced.** Comes from Moravian mission records, including the missionaries' diaries, and the histories written from them, or from one party's own documents or statements. They have not been checked against sources that do not depend on them.
> - **[TBV] To Be Verified.** Seems likely, but nobody has confirmed it yet.

> [!note] Reading this page
> The columns *Built up*, *Held back*, and *Emergent* are the Forum's reading of each law. That reading carries no tag. T stands for Tribes, I for Institutions, M for Markets, and E for Emergent. Residents may read the same laws differently, and the page will change as they say so.

> [!warning] The diary passages
> Rows marked *Diaries* come from new English translations in the [Genadendal Diaries repository](https://github.com/mbh66/genadendal-diaries). They are machine translations that no person has checked yet, so they are a guide to what the diaries say and are not quoted here as finished translations. Each row gives the Utrecht file number and the line numbers, so a reader of the old German or Dutch can check it. The diaries were written by the missionaries, and they show the Khoekhoe through the missionaries' eyes. See [[history/the-genadendal-diaries|The Genadendal Diaries]].

## How to read the tables

Each row is one law or order. *What it did* says what it meant for the valley, where that is known. *Built up* names the form of organisation the law made stronger, and *Held back* names the form it weakened or pushed aside. *Emergent* is filled in only where a law opened room for people to organise together in networks, commons, or partnerships of their own. For most of the valley's history that column is empty, and the emptiness is part of the story. Rows marked *Market signal* record an instrument or decision outside the law that the Forum reads as a turning point.

The tables follow the rulers of the Cape:

| Period | Who made the laws |
| --- | --- |
| Before 1652 | Khoekhoe and San custom |
| 1652 to 1795 | The Dutch East India Company (VOC) |
| 1795 to 1853 | British governors, with Batavian rule from 1803 to 1806 |
| 1853 to 1910 | The Cape Parliament, elected under a franchise open to men of any race who met its property test |
| 1910 to 1948 | The Union of South Africa |
| 1948 to 1990 | The National Party government and the laws of apartheid |
| 1991 to today | The end of apartheid and the democratic Constitution |

## The laws through TIME

This section is the Forum's reading.

**Institutions turning what the Tribes held into Markets.** From 1652 to 1828, almost every law in the tables did the same thing. An Institution, first the company and then the British state, took something the Khoekhoe held in common and made it usable by a market. Grazing land became loan farms in 1714 and priced property in 1813. People's labour became a market fenced in by the Caledon Code of 1809, and children's labour followed in 1812. The diaries show this happening in the valley in small steps: a cattle order read out after church, a certificate a farmer could refuse to sign, a ban on building even a goat shed. The orders came from the Cape, but they were carried out by local men such as Baas Teunis and the field cornet, and they could be withdrawn when the missionaries or the Khoekhoe went to the Cape to argue.

**A window, and what the mission built in it.** Between 1828 and 1909 the law opened doors. Ordinance 50, the end of slavery, school grants, and the Cape franchise gave free people legal room they had never had. The mission used that room to build an Institution of its own: the school, the college of 1838, and the trust grant of 1858 that kept Farm 39 out of the market. The same years also closed doors again. The Masters and Servants laws brought back criminal penalties for workers, and the franchise was narrowed in 1892.

**Race as the rule of the Institutions.** From 1909 the state took over the mission's land and, step by step, made race the rule by which it organised everything. The 1909 Act kept residents on the land without ownership. The 1926 closure of the college pushed the valley's young people back towards farm labour. The Population Registration Act decided who people were, and the Group Areas Act decided where they could live. In Greyton, where plots had been sold as freehold since 1854, the market in land was turned against the coloured families who owned them. In these years Institutions used law to decide who could take part in Markets at all.

**Undoing, and the first room for Emergent.** Since 1991 the laws have mostly undone or tried to repair. Several of them give communities legal forms they can run themselves: the Communal Property Association, the co-operative, water held in trust with a Reserve for rivers, and TRANCRAA's promise that residents will choose who holds their land. These are the only rows in which the Emergent column fills up. They are tools, and the valley has not yet used most of them. The water bond of 2026 is the first sign from the Markets side: capital willing to pay for verified restoration of the catchments the valley's rivers depend on. It may change how the laws already in force, the National Water Act among them, are put to use. The date 19 June 1913 also limits what law can restore, since it leaves the Khoekhoe losses of the centuries before outside the reach of restitution.

## What this page leaves out

- Laws of the northern and eastern frontiers that did not reach the valley directly, such as the commando policy against the San of the 1770s.
- Laws that applied mainly within the African reserves, such as the Glen Grey Act of 1894 and the Bantu Authorities Act of 1951.
- The security laws of the 1960s and later.
- Local by-laws of Greyton's municipality and the rules of the Genadendal boards.

## What is still open

- The legal basis for the closure of the teachers' college in 1926.
- Which law governed Genadendal between 1961 and 1989: the Act of 1909, the Coloured Persons Communal Reserves Act of 1961, or the Rural Coloured Areas Act of 1963.
- Whether residents of Genadendal could vote under the Cape franchise of 1853, and how many were removed from the roll in the 1950s.
- Whether families moved from Greyton under the Group Areas Act have claimed restitution.
- A rule said to date from 1775 that let farmers indenture Khoekhoe children born on their farms until the age of 25. No source for it has been found yet [TBV].
- The text of the Council of Policy ruling of 4 September 1742.
- The law under which the Greyton Nature Reserve was proclaimed in 1977.
- How the Moravian Church, residents, and the Transformasie Komitee read these laws, in their own words.
