Greyton's leiwater ("led water") is a network of open furrows that carries water by gravity from the Gobos River to the village's gardens [MS]. It has served [[villages/greyton|Greyton]] since the village was founded in 1854, and it still works [MS]. It is described as one of the last working heritage irrigation systems of its kind in South Africa [MS]. It is run today by volunteer water bailiffs and street wardens [MS].

## How it works

Water is taken from the Gobos River and led into open furrows that run along Greyton's streets [MS]. It flows by gravity, following the contour of the land, so only plots lying below a furrow can receive it [IC]. Each plot with a leiwater right has a turn, called a *beurt*, at a set time in the schedule [IC]. When its turn comes, the owner opens a small sluice gate and floods the garden [MS].

Today the water alternates, week by week, between two zones fed from the Bo Lei Dam and the Mid Lei Dam [MS]. Water bailiffs keep the schedule, and street wardens report blockages and leaks [MS]. Residents help by keeping to their times and keeping the furrows on their plots clear [MS].

## How it began

The leiwater was part of Greyton's design from the start. [[history/people/herbert-vigne|Herbert Vigne]] laid the village out on the farm Weltevreden, and the surveyor [[history/people/j-g-rietz|J.G. Rietz]] drew a plan of long, narrow plots [IC]. Each plot had room for a house and a strip of land behind it for vegetables, fruit trees, and a few animals [IC]. Water from the mountain made those gardens possible [MS]. When the first plots were sold in 1854, they came with title deeds, water rights, and grazing rights on the commonage [IC]. Residents grew onions, pumpkins, apricots, and pears [MS].

Who dug the first furrows and dams, and how the water rights were written into the first title deeds, has not yet been found [TBV].

## A Cape tradition

Greyton was one of several villages laid out in the Western Cape in the 1850s by dividing a farm into plots with water rights [IC]:

- **Montagu**, subdivided from 1851, gave each plot one hour of irrigation water in its title deed. Its furrows had been dug on the farm before the town existed [IC].
- **Greyton**, whose first plots were sold in 1854 [IC].
- **Stanford**, laid out in 1855, sold its plots "with reference to the availability of water" [IC].

In many towns these furrows have since stopped working [IC]. Prince Albert's furrows have been declared a Provincial Heritage Site [IC]. Stanford's have been nominated for the same protection [IC].

The valley's first water furrow is older than Greyton. In 1738 [[history/people/georg-schmidt|Georg Schmidt]] and his Khoekhoe students dug a furrow to water their gardens at Baviaanskloof, now Genadendal [IC]. In 1797 the mission built a water mill [IC], and in 1899 the Moravian Church bought the mountain farm Langgezocht to protect Genadendal's water sources [MS].

## Water rights and the law

Under older Cape law, water rights could be attached to land, and written into title deeds [IC]. The National Water Act of 1998 changed this. It made the nation's water a public trust held by the state, and water use now needs a licence or another authorisation under the Act [IC]. Water uses that were lawfully exercised in the two years before the Act came into force may continue as "existing lawful uses" until they are licensed [IC]. In Montagu, the old title-deed water rights were replaced by the Act [IC]. How the Act applies to Greyton's leiwater rights has not yet been checked [TBV].

## Why it matters

The leiwater is one of the things that began in this valley and still works [MS]. It keeps gardens green in a dry climate, and keeps alive a way of sharing water by turns that is older than the village [MS]. It depends on the health of the mountain catchment above Greyton, where invasive plants and fire affect how much water reaches the river [TBV]. See [[history/what-began-here|What Began Here]] and [[greyton-conservation-society|Greyton Conservation Society]].
