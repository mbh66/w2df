[[guiding-ethos|Our Guiding Ethos]] sets out the community economy the DevForum works from, and the eight questions every initiative answers. Each of the DevForum's [[priorities/index|priorities]] is paired with one idea from it, and has tests of its own. Each also says what a visitor could find. The champion for each priority uses these tests alongside the eight questions.

## Tourism Development

The story a place tells about itself shapes who it becomes.

This is the priority that opens the valley to people who want to see a community economy at work. Each of the other priorities gives them something to see.

A study of Greyton published in 2009 found that the village's wealth rests on tourism and on people retiring there, and that this gentrification reinforced racial segregation [IC]. More visitors could repeat that pattern. Any difference has to come from who hosts the visitors and where their money goes.

Genadendal's heritage is recorded in the museum as a snapshot of the past. The Emergent form could be seen in the present, in a river being restored and a co-operative being formed. Both could belong to one visit, told through the [[history/the-time-model|TIME]] model. The ward has no working tourism office [MS]. Genadendal turns 300 in 2038 [IC].

The tests:

- Who tells the valley's story, and who gains when visitors pay to hear it?
- Does visitors' money reach Genadendal and the hamlets as well as Greyton?
- Does a visit include work the visitor shares in?
- Are property prices in the villages watched, so the Greyton pattern is seen early if it starts again?

## Agriculture and Food Sovereignty

A garden can feed a household. Feeding the valley needs a processing layer that sits between the farm and the plate: a mill, an oil press, an abattoir, buyers who take produce every week, and equipment people share. We call this the missing middle.

The [[concepts/valley-of-grace-agri-heritage-co-operative|Valley of Grace Agri-Heritage Co-operative]] is the group that will address this missing middle[MS]. 

Elsewhere, two tools have been tried. 
1. In a Participatory Guarantee System, farmers and buyers check each other's farms and vouch for the produce, at less cost than paid certification [TBV]. Large buyers have been slow to accept these labels [TBV]. 
2. In 2009, Brazil required that at least 30 per cent of federal money for school meals buy food straight from family farms [IC]. That law has since been amended [TBV].

A visitor would find food grown here, and could follow it from the field to the plate. The proposed co-operative plans farm visits and meals made from members' produce, sold through a shared farm shop [MS].

The tests:

- Does valley food reach valley households first, including those who can't pay full price?
- Who owns the shared equipment, and who decides when it's used?
- Can growers vouch for each other's produce in a way a buyer will accept?
- When visitors eat here, does the money reach the growers?

Abattoirs fall under the Meat Safety Act 40 of 2000 [IC]. Any plan for one starts with that law.

## Business Development and Local Procurement

Valley businesses sell to the wider economy, and the valley trades more with itself.

In addition to selling produce to tourists and visitors, the valley can also trade with itself in ways that doesn't need money. In commitment pooling, people pool promises of what they can offer, such as vegetables or a day's work, and draw on the pool for what they need [MS]. The full idea is on the [BioConomy wiki](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth/concepts/commitment-pooling). [[concepts/the-palmiet-principle|The Palmiet Principle]] proposes a pool like this for each stretch of river.

The best-studied case in Africa is the Sarafu network in Kenya. Researchers outside the organisation that runs it studied its records for 2020 and 2021. They found the vouchers mostly stayed local and changed hands many times, with savings groups at the centre [IC]. Stokvels (savings clubs whose members pay in and take turns to draw) came up at the [[civic/governance/between-meetings/2026-11-14-minutes|site visit in Genadendal]] on 14 November 2026. One view there was that stokvels aren't part of the valley's local culture, and that trust may be an issue [MS].

A local currency token raises legal questions. Since October 2022, anyone who advises on crypto assets as a regular business, or acts for others in buying and selling them, needs a licence from the Financial Sector Conduct Authority [IC]. The Reserve Bank and other regulators say crypto assets aren't money or legal tender [IC]. Our understanding is that a voucher you can spend only with named valley suppliers, and can't change back into rand, doesn't break any of these rules. We will have to seek legal advice before anything is issued.

Visitors could try a pool for the length of their stay. They might put in a morning's work on a river and draw a meal from a member farm. This is an idea to test, and it raises the same legal questions as a token.

The tests:

- When a rand is spent here, how many valley hands does it pass through before it leaves?
- Does it give a valley supplier a fair chance at work that the municipality and large businesses buy?
- If it works like money, can it be changed into rand? If so, who has given legal advice?

## Education and Skills

People change before structures do, so formation comes first.

South Africa's first teachers' training college opened at Genadendal in 1838 [IC]. Henk Swanepoel of The Shift brought the Formation First model to the valley at the [[civic/governance/between-meetings/2026-10-07-formation-first-presentation|Formation First Economy Presentation]] on 7 October 2026, and again at [[civic/governance/between-meetings/2026-10-29-the-shift-in-greyton|The Shift in Greyton]] on 29 October [MS]. It asks whether the people in a place are ready to carry what development brings [MS]. The DevForum's research found strong histories of the Nordic folk high schools, and little data from Africa on how this kind of learning turns out [MS]. Work on this priority includes [[concepts/vorming|Vorming]] and the [[concepts/valley-of-grace-learning-campus|Valley of Grace Learning Campus]].

Visitors would come to learn by working. The Learning Campus proposal would bring learners from outside the valley to spend their school years restoring a catchment beside young people who live in it. Vorming adds exchanges with partner schools abroad.

The tests:

- Does the learning happen on the valley's own land, in its own work?
- Does it count what adults already know?
- Before money arrives, has anyone asked whether the people involved are ready to look after what it builds?
- When visitors come to learn, do valley residents teach, and are they paid for it?

## Conservation and Environment

Work with the land's own ways of holding water and building soil.

Invasive alien trees take water that would otherwise reach the rivers, and a 2016 study estimated the losses for each catchment in South Africa [TBV]. Work on this priority includes [[concepts/restoring-the-river|Restoring the River]], [[concepts/ridge-to-river-to-table|Ridge to River to Table]], and [[concepts/the-palmiet-principle|The Palmiet Principle]].

Holding water high in a catchment can leave less for people downstream [TBV]. Farmers and towns below share the same river.

A visitor could walk a stretch of river with the people restoring it, and help for a morning. Under [[concepts/the-palmiet-principle|The Palmiet Principle]], a tourism business could adopt a stretch, as [[long-term-goal|Our Long-Term Goal]] suggests.

The tests:

- Who watches the water, and are they paid when it improves?
- Is care for a river seen and recorded, even when nobody pays for it?
- Has anyone checked what the work does to people downstream?
- Can visitors help with the work without getting in its way?
