## Preface; May and June 1803



Diary of the Hottentot congregation in Baviaanskloof. From May 1803 to February 1804.

Several of our Hottentots, especially the older ones, find it very hard to commit anything from God's Word to memory, because they know little Dutch. Yet we wish that those who want to advance in the graces of the congregation and grow in the knowledge of Jesus Christ should be able to do so. It was therefore thought good to call the candidates for Holy Communion together on their own from time to time in future, and to talk with them about Holy Communion, so that in this way too they might gain a truer understanding of it. Brother Rose made a start on 2 May, and the Hottentots showed themselves very grateful for this instruction.

On the 16th a young man of the Kaffer nation [1] came to us and earnestly asked permission to live with us. We granted it, at least for a time, and wished that he might be won for the Saviour.

At the speaking before Holy Communion [2] this month, several brothers and sisters confessed that they were in the habit of looking too much at others, and that this kept them from making greater progress in grace themselves. One sister said: "My heart is still full of what I enjoyed at the last Communion. I often go off by myself, so that my heart stays open to the Saviour and I do not lose him."

On 24 May several Hottentot families, 24 persons in all, arrived here on their way back from the camp [3]. They asked permission to stay with us. We therefore spoke with them at length in the church and set before them all the rules in force among us, and they promised with a handshake to keep them.

On 30 May, the second day of Pentecost, 6 adults and 1 child were baptised into the death of Jesus, and 13 persons became candidates for baptism. When we told the former a few days before that the Saviour had granted them the grace of holy baptism, they were deeply humbled and glad. In the days that followed they told us plainly of their hearts' longing to be cleansed from all their sins by the blood of Jesus, and we took the opportunity to put it to them that coming to the Saviour was not enough: they must now also stay faithful to him. The baptism itself stirred all our Hottentots, and one could see clearly that the Holy Spirit had been especially at work in hearts during these days of Pentecost. On the day after the baptism the Hottentots came to us in great numbers and told us of their wish to advance in the graces of the congregation. Among them were some who have lived here a long time; we would be very glad if their hour had also come to wake from the sleep of sin. The children, too, came to us one after another, asking us to remember them, because they would gladly become children of the Saviour.

About this time our Hottentots learned that runaway slaves were hiding in our district and had already stolen an ox in the veld, so several of our young men went out to pursue these robbers. They came home again, however, without having found them.

On 20 May [4] another 37 Hottentots, men, women and children, came to us from the camp, several of whom had lived here before. Since they very much wanted to stay with us, we spoke with them thoroughly. We explained our arrangements to them fully and told them that anyone who did not wish to live by them had better leave again at once; but whoever was willing, with God's help, to keep them should give us his hand on it. At once they all stood up to give us their hands, and one could feel that it came from their hearts.

For the rest, we had few visitors from outside in these months, either from Cape Town or from places nearer by, so that we mostly lived quietly by ourselves.

## July 1803



On 11 and 12 July we had bitter cold here, so that there was ice in the mornings. On the 13th, however, rain came, to the great joy of the Hottentots, who could now plough their land; it is high time for that now.

At the speaking of the baptised and the candidates for baptism there were several lovely declarations, full of the sense of their sin [1]. One of the candidates for baptism said: "Until now things have looked very bad in my heart. This is already my third year as a candidate for baptism, and I must confess that I had lost from my heart the grace that had come to me. When I heard that we were to be spoken with, I was very frightened and thought: 'Oh God, how do things stand with you?' Then I fell at the Saviour's feet and begged him to have mercy on me anew."

A woman candidate for baptism said: "The Holy Spirit set my lost condition, and all the sins I have committed since my youth, so plainly before my eyes that I could think nothing else than that I must be lost for ever. But Jesus revealed himself to me as my Saviour and gave the comfort of his death to my poor heart, so that now I can believe that he let his hands and feet be pierced on the tree of the cross for my sins too."

A girl said: "There must be great corruption in my heart, for at one moment I feel disobedience towards my parents, at another I am plagued by pride, because I often want to be better than others. I grieve the Saviour so often that I am sometimes afraid he will cast me off in the end. But when I ask his forgiveness for all the bad I find in myself, I feel his love in my heart again."

One of the baptised said: "Every day I ask the Saviour to keep me close to him, so that I may always feel his peace in my heart. I am old and do not know when the thread of my life will be cut. With that thought I have only the one wish, that the Saviour would show me grace and bring me one step nearer to him." (By these words he meant to make known his wish to become a candidate for Communion.) He added: "I am not worthy of it, for I am a great sinner, but all the more do I need grace."

Another said: "I am no longer as simple and childlike towards the Saviour as I was for a long time. That is why I no longer feel the nearness of Jesus in my heart as I did, and notice more clearly that I keep getting worse. So I have asked the Saviour to have mercy on me anew and give me his peace in my heart, because I do mean to live for him alone in this world."

An excluded baptised woman [2] said: "I have broken the covenant I made with the Saviour in baptism, and by my sins I have crucified Jesus anew. The Spirit of God often knocked at my door, but for a long time I went about as if dead and gave him no hearing. Now I see what harm I did myself by leaving the Saviour and giving myself over again to the service of sin, and I ask the Saviour to forgive me my strayings."

Another woman, who had stayed for a time with her mother on a farm, said: "By going away from here I did myself much harm [3]. When I came back and went to the meetings again, I noticed that there was no feeling left in my heart and that I had grown cold and indifferent towards the Saviour, though before I had lived in the enjoyment of his love. In the past, when a bad thought came to me, I confessed it at once to the Saviour and to my teachers, and then my heart was light and glad again. But now that I had lost my simplicity and the peace of God, I was anxious and afraid to admit to my teachers the bad things springing up in my heart. Yet the Holy Spirit gave me no rest until I turned to the Saviour in all my distress and threw myself at his pierced feet. There I found forgiveness, and now I know again what I have in the Saviour."

A girl of 10, who had been baptised as a small child, also came to Sister Rose [4] and wished to be spoken with. When the sister said that she was now speaking with the adult baptised and not with the children, the girl said with many tears: "Oh, I poor lost child must come too and say how things are with me. I do not really feel the Saviour's love in my heart, and that is why I am crying. I am afraid my poor soul may be lost, even though I am baptised." She asked whether the Saviour would have mercy on her again. She was assured that if she turned to him as a poor child he would certainly hear her, and then she said: "I will ask him with my whole heart to let me feel his love again."

Towards the end of July we had very cold weather, and our mountains were covered with snow. This cold is very hard on us European Brethren and Sisters in this country every time, and it always brings various ailments with it.

On the 29th, to our great joy, a Hottentot returning from Cape Town brought us some congregation news [5] and several letters from our dear Brethren and Sisters in Zeist.

## August 1803



On 7 August we received the sad news that one of our schoolboys, who was going to the Cape with a farmer as leader of the oxen, had the misfortune on the way to fall from the wagon; the wheels went over his body and he died on the spot.

On the 8th a 3-year-old girl whom we had baptised fell asleep. A few days before her end she said to her mother: "I am not afraid of death, for then I shall go to the dear Saviour, and he will surely take me in." Another time she said: "Don't hold me back, for I must see the dear Saviour himself, and he will surely come soon and call me to him."

On 13 August 5 adults and 2 children were baptised into the death of Jesus. When we asked them about their intentions, all of them had said that it was the wish of their hearts to live and die for the Saviour. In the evening we partook of the body and blood of the Lord in Holy Communion, a grace that two persons shared with us for the first time. Three were present as confirmands and 5 as candidates.

On the 18th we had a very unpleasant scene. Three months earlier our herdsmen had found a strange horse on our fields. We gave it to a Hottentot, a brother named Petrus Blesier, to look after until the owner came forward. At the same time, as the law of the land here requires, we reported to the Landdrost in Stellenbosch that a strange horse was with us. Soon afterwards we received a letter from a farmer who, with many harsh words, accused the said Petrus Blesier of stealing his horse. We sent word that he should come and fetch his horse himself, and that we would then speak with him. Now he came, together with a field corporal, and once he had properly proved that he owned the horse, it was handed over to him at once. But then we demanded that he declare the Hottentot Petrus Blesier an honest man and admit that he himself had written what was untrue. At first he would not agree, because, he said, it was only a Hottentot and no Christian. But we told him that if he did not give this declaration in writing we would know what to do, and that he would find out that the laws of the land protect the innocent. So at last he had to comply, and we were very glad to see the matter settled this way without further trouble.

It is a very common thing for the farmers to allow themselves such unjust accusations against the Hottentots: whenever thefts or other bad things happen on a farm, a Hottentot must always be the culprit. We cannot help taking the part of our people in such cases, just as, on the other hand, we are very strict with all who really are guilty of such things, and send them away from us as the case requires.

After very violent thunderstorms on 23 August, we had very cold and wet weather in the last days of this month.

## September 1803



It cleared up again on 3 September, but on the 13th we again had such cold rain that the mountains near us were covered in snow.

On 17 September one of our communicant sisters went happily home. Since being admitted to Holy Communion she had kept watch with a faithful heart over the grace given her, and the other brothers and sisters loved and valued her for her childlike, believing attachment to Jesus. In her illness she was visited often, and was always found turned towards the Saviour and full of joy that she would soon have the grace of seeing her Redeemer and being with him for ever.

For some time we had noticed with sorrow that our schoolchildren gave way to frivolity, in school and in the meetings alike, and allowed themselves all kinds of bad behaviour. Since all the warnings so far had borne no fruit, Br. Rose had to try something in the school. After the children had recited to him, with great pleasure, what they had learned, he put it to them kindly but firmly how much trouble their teachers took to acquaint them in their youth with their God and Creator, and to impress on their hearts how much it had cost the Saviour that they could become his dear children. The children listened to this in great silence, and Br. Rose then told them that if they went on behaving as they had until now, grieving the Saviour and us, we would be obliged to close the school and stop the children's meetings. He asked them to tell him quite simply and honestly what they now meant to do. When the children promised to be obedient in future, Br. Rose said that those for whom this was the truth, and no empty promise, should stand up and give us their hands on it; but anyone who did not mean to give the Saviour their whole heart should stay seated. At once the children hurried in floods of tears to Brothers Rose and Kohrhammer. Each wanted to give the first handshake, and the little ones, who were held back, raised both hands high just to be seen. One could feel that they meant it in earnest, and this event had good results.

In these days we suffered the pain of seeing one of our communicants fall so deep into open sin that he had to leave the congregation. His unfaithful disposition had not been wholly hidden from us, but he knew how to do his evil deeds so much in the dark that we could prove nothing against him, and when we spoke to him he used to deny everything. It was the first case of its kind here, and it filled all the brothers and sisters in the whole congregation with sorrow. The man had taken his son of about 16 with him by force. But as they were nearing the capital [1], the son said: "Father, I cannot stay separated from God and his Word. I will turn back and go to Baviaanskloof." To which the father answered: "Well then, go where you like."

On the 20th [2] several more Hottentot families, most of whom had lived here before, came back to us from the camp. At their request they were given permission to stay, on their promise to keep our rules. However willing these people are to confirm that promise with a handshake, they often leave us at a loss, because several of them were without our care for six or seven years, and in that time everything they had learned from the Word of God has slipped from their memories and their hearts.

On the 25th a widowed sister fell asleep, who had been a communicant since 1799. Among us she proved a true sister: her unfeigned love for the Saviour, her honest conduct towards everyone, and her patient endurance in her long and painful illness were an encouragement and a blessing to others. More than six years ago, going barefoot, she had trodden on the skeleton of a snake and received what seemed a trifling injury. But the poison had nevertheless entered her body, and in spite of every remedy tried, she gradually lost the use of several limbs. Whenever she was visited during this illness she declared that she was going to the Saviour as a poor but pardoned sinner, and that we could be fully comforted about her.

For some days a rumour had been spreading here that war had broken out again in Europe [3], and now, sadly, we learned that it was entirely true. For on the 25th Captain Lesueur arrived here, accompanied by Field Cornet Otto. The captain handed us a written order from the government, to this effect: Governor Janssens had instructed Captain Lesueur to go to our settlement and recruit a volunteer corps of Hottentots for military service. We were therefore asked to put no obstacle in the captain's way in carrying out this commission, and to support him in every way. At the same time we were assured that due care would be taken to preserve the good morals we have instilled in the Hottentots.

So at the end of the sermon it was announced that all the males in the church should stay there, and that all those in their houses should come at once when the bell was rung as a signal. When all were assembled, Br. Rose first explained the purpose of the meeting to them; then Captain Lesueur read out the government's order, and its content was explained sentence by sentence until they all declared that they had understood everything. Finally they were asked to think carefully about what they had heard, and then to give the captain their views one by one. No one was to leave here before he had given his answer. After the midday meal Field Cornet Otto rode home again, while the captain sent an express to ask Field Cornet Linde to come here, presumably because this man knows our people well and could be of good service to the captain in his business.

Mr Linde arrived the next day, and on the 27th all the males present here, old and young, were asked for their decision by the captain and Field Cornet Linde in the presence of Br. Rose. It turned out that 22 men enlisted, among them one communicant, two baptised men and two candidates for baptism. They asked only that provision be made for the upkeep of their wives and children, who number 40 persons, and the captain promised to speak to the government on their behalf.

For us this whole affair was very unpleasant, all the more because we had to fear that it will not stop at this first attempt, since the government wants to get a considerable number of reliable men from among our baptised, who could be appointed as non-commissioned officers in the Hottentot corps. For our part we did everything possible to convince the government that we do not wish to stand in the way of its purpose. For our own security Br. Rose had written down everything that had happened, and at our request Messrs Lesueur and Linde confirmed this document with their signatures. In the following days these gentlemen left, each with a part of the recruited Hottentots. We sent the recruits off with heartfelt exhortations about their new station and calling, and asked them not to lose sight of the teaching they had received from us out of God's Word, and to live by it. Many took leave with tears in their eyes, and at this sight we too were so moved that we had to go off by ourselves and let our tears flow freely.

To do everything in our power, on our side, for the good of these Hottentots, Br. Rose had written to Governor Janssens, congratulating him on his return from the Kaffer country [4] and commending most earnestly to his charity and care the wives and children left behind by the Hottentots who had volunteered for service, who are now entirely without support.

1. *Hauptstadt*: Cape Town.
2. The diary gives only the day for this passage and the next two. The month is presumably September, since the passages fall between 17 September and 4 October. Two events are dated "the 25th".
3. War between Britain and France resumed in May 1803, and the Batavian Republic, which then governed the Cape, was drawn in on the French side.
4. *Kaffern-Lande*: the Xhosa country beyond the eastern border of the colony. Governor Janssens toured the eastern frontier in 1803.

## October 1803



On 4 October one of our Hottentot sisters told us that the wife of Field Cornet Otto, who lies mortally ill, had talked with her about the state of her soul. On that occasion the sister had testified that the death of Jesus is the only ground of our salvation and our only comfort in life and in death.

At the speaking of the communicants before Holy Communion one of them said: "My heart is still full of what the Saviour let me enjoy at the last Communion, and now I am called again. Poor me, what shall I say? I would rather hide than show myself before so good a Lord as my Saviour is." Another said: "I have so many visitors in my heart that I no longer know what to do." Asked what these visitors were, he answered: "Nothing but worries about my body, and over them I forget my poor soul. I can tell that the enemy of my soul wants to darken my faith, but my heart cries to the Saviour for help, and he will not leave his child in the lurch."

On the 15th and 16th we had a visit from several ladies and gentlemen from Cape Town, who looked round our place and also attended the meetings. Among them were a Mr van Oudtshoorn and a Danish sea captain named Sönnigsen. On the second of these days Field Cornet Linde also arrived with another gentleman, bringing us a written order from the Governor, which said that the cornet should try, if possible, to recruit all the single men as volunteers. Arrangements were made at once for all the males, married and unmarried, to be presented to Mr Linde. But since this time only volunteers were expressly wanted, and it therefore had to be left to the Hottentots themselves whether to enlist or not, none of them, in spite of all the promises, felt moved to take service. All declared that they were willing, as soon as the alarm shot was fired, to march out man by man in defence of the country, as the laws of the land require. Finally the names of all the young men fit for service were written down and the list was sent to the government. The more we saw what trust the government places in our people, and how much it wants to take a great many of them into its service, the more we find ourselves at a loss; we put our hope in the Saviour's help alone, and earnestly commended ourselves and our Hottentots to him.

On 30 October we received a very friendly letter from Governor Janssens, in which, answering Br. Rose's letter to him, he declared most graciously and in a fatherly way that he would provide for the upkeep of the wives and children of the Hottentots in the service of the state. He adds that so far he has been very well satisfied with the people from Baviaanskloof. He sent us this letter by two of our baptised men, who have been appointed corporals and had been given twelve days' leave, together with orders to encourage more Hottentots to take service.

## November 1803



On 2 November the unbaptised wife of a Hottentot brother, who is a communicant, departed this life. Although she had lived here for many years and heard God's Word, no one ever noticed that it had made any impression on her heart; she lived on quite unconcerned about herself until at last she saw that she was going to die. Then she began to seek grace and mercy from Jesus, which, as we trust in faith, she will also have found. Her husband and her children, who go to school, sang her some verses before her end, and she died while they sang.

On the 4th four of our Hottentots who have taken military service again left for the camp. Among them was one candidate for baptism. Each of them was spoken to suitably at parting, and what they have heard here was laid on their hearts once more.

On the 5th a man named Carl Schmidt arrived here on his way from Cape Town to the lower country. He told us that he was born in Lübbenau in Lower Lusatia, had come to the Brethren's congregation at Ebersdorf in 1780, and had been admitted to Holy Communion there. He later went to Gnadenfeld and left the congregation in 1792. After that he enlisted as a soldier in Holland and came to Cape Town in 1793. Now he lives with his wife and child on Krumbeck's River [1], seven miles below Swellendam. He regretted the step he took in his youth in leaving the congregation [2], and said: "Outwardly I suffer no hardship and no want, but oh, I feel that the hand of God finds me even here in Africa. It is hard to struggle against that hand, and a wounded conscience is never silent." He attended the Sunday meetings and took leave with many tears.

On the 6th a large party belonging to the newly founded African Society [3] arrived on a visit, accompanied by Mr Wesel Wessels. They all attended our meetings, to their edification, as they said. Part of the party left again on the 8th, and on the 9th Mr Wessels [4] followed them with his wife and children. He was very grateful for the blessing he had enjoyed here.

On 13 November an excluded baptised woman departed this life, who had lain painfully ill for two years and had served others as a warning example. Soon after she had become unfaithful to the grace given her in baptism and fallen into sin, she was struck by apoplexy, which robbed her of hearing and sight and left her entirely crippled. We gave her many charitable gifts; but since no one could talk with her, one always had to leave her with a sorrowful heart, and could do nothing for her soul except commend her to the mercy of God.

On the 22nd Field Cornet Otto informed us that it had pleased the Lord of life and death to take his wife to himself. At the same time he asked that a couple of the Brethren would do him the kindness of accompanying her body to its resting place. So Brothers Kohrhammer and Kühnel travelled there and were received very warmly by Mr Otto, who said he was most grateful that we had not refused his request. He added that his late wife had always felt great love for the Brethren and Sisters and had received much blessing for her heart among us. He himself now thought quite differently from before. In the past, when he heard people speak of the pardoned and the unpardoned, the converted and the unconverted, he had taken everything said about it for mere fables and foolery; but at his wife's end he had been vividly convinced that there is a great difference between believing and unbelieving souls, between the pardoned and the unpardoned, and that one can die blessed only in Jesus Christ. He wished that this conviction might never leave his mind again; but, he added, he felt no true peace in his heart and could not yet believe in the comfort of the Gospel. The Brothers told him that God often uses such means to convince us of our natural unbelief. Then one learns to understand the words rightly: "Lord, help thou mine unbelief!"

On the 24th Field Cornet Linde visited us. He has been commissioned to buy the grain needed for the wives and children of the Hottentots in service. The government had fixed one mud [5], or almost one Dresden Scheffel, a week for twelve women, and the same for 24 children, and we were asked to supervise and to see to the distribution with Mr Linde's help. Since no rye was to be had anywhere in the district at present, barley was given out instead. For the rest, we, like those who benefited from this kindness, were heartily grateful for the generous care of our beloved authorities.

On the 26th, after an extraordinarily long drought, we again had a refreshing rain, which relieved not only the dryness but also the plague of caterpillars, which had begun to do great damage to the garden and field crops. We gave our heartfelt thanks for this blessing to the Giver of all good things.

Towards the end of this month many of our Hottentots, men, women and children, left here to help the farmers with the harvest. Since we knew how busy the enemy is on such occasions in leading the Hottentots into sin, especially through drink, we took care to put it to them firmly what harm they could do to their souls if they did not keep the Saviour in their hearts and before their eyes.

## December 1803 and the year's figures



On 3 December a government messenger informed us that General Commissioner De Mist would honour us with a visit the next day. So all the preparations our circumstances allowed were made to receive so high a person in authority. Brothers Rose and Kühnel, with some Hottentots, rode to the boundary of our land to welcome the party there, and on the 4th, towards evening, we saw our friends arrive: Mr De Mist, his son and daughter, and the son of Governor Janssens, with their retinue, 24 persons in all. A number of children had gathered in our yard and welcomed the party as they dismounted by singing some verses. The whole party listened with moved hearts, and the General Commissioner expressed his particular pleasure. The party then inspected our whole establishment. The General Commissioner inquired closely into how we are organised, inwardly and outwardly, and expressed his satisfaction, adding that the mission would always enjoy every protection and support from the government. When the party left again, which was on the 5th, Brothers Kohrhammer and Kühnel accompanied them back to the boundary of our land.

Towards the middle of this month all the inhabitants here were listed, as usual, house by house, so that the register could be sent to the authorities at the end of the year. On the 14th we received from Cape Town a packet of congregation news and letters from the fatherland; the letters were dated 1794 and 1795. The packet had been 8¼ years on the way before it reached our hands. In what part of the world it was during all that time we do not know. Still, we were glad to receive it at all.

On the 24th a great crowd of strangers, Christians as well as heathen, came for Christmas Eve and Christmas, and on Christmas Day the number of visitors grew still larger, so that our church was almost completely full. Immediately after the sermon many went home again, since many are still busy with the harvest. The rest also stayed for the second day of Christmas, and we hope that many enjoyed a blessing for their hearts here in these days. We certainly have reason to be glad and thankful that with so great a crowd of people, of such different kinds, everything goes so quietly and in such good order. Many of the Christians lodge with the Hottentots and are glad to find any shelter at all. The rest sleep on their wagons or in a tent, so that we now have little inconvenience from it.

On the 27th 7 privates and 1 corporal from among our Hottentots in military service arrived here on a few days' leave. With them came a letter from Captain Lesueur, in which he shows very friendly feelings towards us and our Hottentots. We also learned from it that on our recommendation he had considered several of our people and appointed them corporals.

On the 30th and 31st one party of visitors after another arrived by wagon, on horseback and on foot, to see out the year with us. In both meetings, in which we closed the old year and entered the new, our church was crammed with people, all very quiet and attentive. At the prayer on our knees with which we entered the new year, many tears of thanks and of repentance were shed by strangers and inhabitants alike. This time there were many, especially Christians, who were here for the first time in their lives, several of whom live 3 or 4 days' journey away. Because, as they said themselves, they had heard very different judgements about us, some good and some bad, they had now come to see and hear for themselves how things really were, and they declared themselves entirely satisfied.

In the year 1803, 24 adults and 14 children, 38 persons in all, received holy baptism; 28 persons became candidates for baptism and 14 persons candidates for Communion. 10 persons were admitted to Holy Communion. In all, 25 children were born, 13 of them to baptised parents. 37 persons died, 16 of them baptised. 2 couples were married. ## January 1804



1804.

The year 1804.

The sermon on New Year's Day was so well attended that for lack of benches many had to sit on the ground. In the afternoon most of the visitors went home again. Most of them took leave of us with tears, spoke of their joy at their visit, and asked us to remember them in prayer before our dear Lord. Some women who are troubled about the salvation of their souls were especially moved, and we commended to them Jesus' love for sinners. Several black slaves also came to us to say goodbye and to give thanks for the words they had heard here.

Since we have had no letters from the fatherland for a year and a day, and so no text books [1] for this year either, we had to use an old little text book. Although God's Word always stays the same, we were very sorry not to be edified day by day by one and the same Word of God together with our Brethren and Sisters in other parts of the world. As we have no almanac either, we are in the same position as all our neighbours: we did not know on which day Easter and Pentecost fall.

On 6 January, the Festival of the Heathen [2], we first held a general morning blessing at 9 o'clock with all our Hottentots, in which we commended the whole Unity of the Brethren to the Saviour's blessing, and especially all the congregations gathered from among the heathen. At 10 o'clock there was a sermon, and in the afternoon a woman was baptised into the death of Jesus. The day before, another woman had received holy baptism on her sickbed. In the evening we partook of Holy Communion and so closed this day of blessing, on which our Saviour graciously showed himself to us as the God of light and the comfort of the heathen. Many tears of thanks and of repentance showed that his dear presence was powerfully felt in all our meetings, and, to our encouragement, we became aware that he hears the prayers that all our Brethren and Sisters send to him for us on this day.

On the 11th the Hottentots were told that, since the harvest was now mostly over, the meetings and the schools would again be held in their usual order, and all were very grateful for this.

On the 14th we had a visit from old Mrs Morgelin, whose son is our neighbour. She was very friendly towards us. Besides her, one of our nearest neighbours, Mr Badmus of Guldenhaus, attended our meeting. He came to our church for the first time on New Year's night, and liked it so much then that he now comes often.

About this time one of our sheep was torn in the night, probably by a jackal. The Hottentots hunted these animals in the following days and killed three of them, and later one was even caught in our garden. Even so we found we had to keep a watchman at night, because these animals were beginning to do great damage to our vines.

On the 19th Magdalena [3], who had been baptised on 5 January, fell asleep. She was one of the first to come here when our Brethren arrived in Baviaanskloof. She heard the Word of God, but it could not take firm root, because, following the Hottentot custom, she moved from one place to another. But the merciful Friend of sinners left her no peace. She came back from time to time, and at last was struck by a severe illness that obliged her to stay here for good. She became so paralysed in all her limbs that her food had to be put into her mouth. In this condition we visited her often, and since, as she said herself, she was very afraid of death, we urged her to think of the salvation of her soul. But only on 4 January of this year did the hour come when she was so powerfully convinced of her wretched condition that she truly knew what it means to be a sinner. In this deep feeling of her misery she turned to him who died for sinners, and found grace, forgiveness of sins and rest for her poor soul. She told of this blessed change with much joy, and now also laid open her whole past life, with all the sins she had committed. At the same time she asked earnestly to be washed of all her sins in the bath of holy baptism. When her request was granted the next day, she showed with much gratitude to the Saviour how well it now stood with her heart, and then gave a fine testimony of the state of an unpardoned and of a pardoned soul. She said: "When my heart condemned me and my sins went over my head, I forgot the sickness of my body in the misery of my soul. This distress brought me so far that I writhed and twisted and threw myself from one place to another. When my children asked me what was wrong, I could not tell them the grief of my soul. That was my condition while I was still unpardoned [4]. But when the blood of Jesus cried for mercy for me too, all fear of death and damnation vanished. Now I know that Jesus is my Saviour too; I am glad that I shall see him soon, and I count bodily death a gain." She remained in this frame of heart, to the blessing and edification of many, until she died.

On the 21st Mr Desch of Stellenbosch arrived on a visit with a party from the warm baths [5]. He is one of the directors of the African Society, and in the early days he showed our Brethren much friendship by working with great zeal for the Saviour's cause. The party spent the night with us and the next day attended all our meetings.

#### Notes

1. *Losungsbücher*: the Moravian Daily Texts (Watchwords), a book of a verse for each day, read on the same day by congregations worldwide.
2. *Heidenfest*: the Moravian name for Epiphany, 6 January, kept as a festival of the missions among non-Christian peoples.
3. This Magdalena was baptised as an adult on 5 January 1804, so she is a different woman from the Magdalena (Vehettge Tikkuie) whom Georg Schmidt baptised decades earlier. The two should not be confused.
4. The normalised text closes the quotation after "the grief of my soul" and then continues in the first person. The English treats the whole speech as one quotation.
5. *aus dem warmen Bade*: probably the hot springs at present-day Caledon. To be confirmed.

## February 1804 and the signatures



On 5 February Field Cornet Otto brought us a written order from the government, requiring all inhabitants of the country to make a conscientious return of what they had sown, of its yield, and of all the livestock they own. Every head of a household is to confirm this return with his signature and to add how large his family is. A fine of 500 Rixdollars was fixed for those who did not prove themselves loyal subjects in this. Since we are not farmers, and must buy our grain, drink and slaughter stock, we did not know how far this proclamation bound us, and Mr Otto could not advise us on it either. We therefore thought it best to write to the Landdrost at Stellenbosch and ask his opinion on the matter.

Extract from the diary of Baviaanskloof, from June to the end of November 1804.

Just as we had an extraordinary drought in the past months, so it began again this month. It puts our whole district in great difficulty: nothing can be sown, because the ground is like rock, and the poor cattle cannot find their food either and in many places must die of hunger. This drought lasted until [1]

On 9 and 10 June there was so violent a storm that it threatened to tear off our roofs and several others, and the church roof especially, entirely, so the church was shut. All the men hurried to help, by day the roof [2], and the faithful Saviour let the means succeed and preserved our building, so that we suffered only slight damage, which was soon repaired. For this we thanked the Saviour.

On the 13th we held Holy Communion, and also had the joy of receiving letters from Br. von Forestier in Berthelsdorf and Br. Schreiter in Zeist, which the Governor forwarded to us.

On 14 July we were visited by Captain Weimere, who is coming from China and bound for Emden. We would gladly have sent Brethren [3] with him to Zeist, but because of the war he had misgivings about taking them. He did, however, ask to borrow our register of personal records, which he wanted to show in Zeist as proof that he had been here.

On 14 July our friend Peter Ditoy came to visit us with his brother and their wives and children. Daniel Ditoy visited our old Simeon Rokkers and some other Hottentots who had often worked [for them] [4], and said to Simeon: "Since I grew up in the house under your care, you are still dear to me; but who would have thought that you would live to be so old?" Simeon answered with tears in his eyes: "Yes, Baas, the merciful God has done this. He has spared me so that I should come to know his Son Jesus, my Saviour, and through him be washed of my sins with his blood in holy baptism. This good fortune [has come to] the Baas [5], to change my evil heart. But Baas must not think that everything is now done. No: sin still lies at the door, and old as I am, I must still see and feel that much is still lacking in me; and when I forget to pray, sin comes in at the door again," and so on. Daniel Ditoy looked at the old man with astonished eyes and went away from him with tears in his eyes.

On the 19th a certain A. Mast Osworter [6] came, and on hearing that some had been called to become candidates for baptism and some to holy baptism, she said: "The evening before, I went into my garden, threw myself on my face and said: 'Dear Saviour, even if I and my house are so ungrateful, do not let others in the congregation suffer for it!' And now I see that the Saviour does not despise the prayer of poor sinners."

On 28 July we had extraordinarily bitter cold, so that in the night the mountains were entirely covered with snow and there was ice. The month of August went on and on with harsh, cold weather and endless downpours, so that the rivers could no longer be crossed. The rain had now softened the parched ground again, so that the plough could get into the earth once more. The dearth continued.

On the 13th two women named Erdmath and Sara were baptised into the death of Jesus, and in the evening we held Holy Communion, at which four persons looked on as candidates. On the 24th Brother and Sister Kohrhammer set out for the Cape, to the Hottentots stationed in the camp. On the 27th we received from Commandant Linde, on the government's account, three wagonloads of grain to distribute to the wives and children of those Hottentots whose husbands and fathers are in military service in the camp. A load of grain costs 72 Rixdollars.

On 3 September we had news of the safe arrival of Brother and Sister Kohrhammer in the camp, where they were received with much love by the officer commanding there, Mr LeSueur, and afterwards by General Commissioner De Mist and Governor Janssens.

The preacher Voos [7], formerly stationed at Roodezand in Africa, who went to England two years ago, arrived in Cape Town a few days ago by way of Copenhagen with three other missionaries, among them Mr Ringeltaube. He had asked for permission to stay here again, but did not receive it, and will go with his wife and two missionaries to Ceylon, while Ringeltaube goes with three missionaries to Madras.

Br. Rose also received greetings from a soldier in garrison at the Cape named Johann Christoph Becher, a native of Suhl in Thuringia. He was brought up as a child in the institution at Ebersdorf, learned weaving there, was received into the congregation and admitted to Holy Communion, but then left the congregation, led astray by his stepfather. Later he travelled to Holland on business, fell there into the hands of the soul-sellers [8], was robbed of all he had and sold as a soldier, and was sent here when the Cape was newly taken over. He sought out Br. Rose while Rose was at the Cape and poured out his heart to him, more with tears than with words. Among other things his letter says: "The state of my soul is most sad and dangerous, as remorse and despair rage in my heart. Only anxious sighs and tears to my dear Saviour bring my oppressed heart some relief. Oh, if only I had taken your kind and gracious parting words with me, perhaps I would not have come into this wretched state. Pray for me to him from whom all help comes and who knows our need."

On the 5th [9] a dispatch rider from the Governor's office announced the following persons for a midday meal with us: Captain Paravicini di Capelli, the Governor's adjutant; the Governor's son with his tutor, Mr Lichtenstein; Captain of Horse Harting; Lieutenant de Labat; and Lieutenant Meyer, with three dragoons. They stayed here a few hours and dined with us, and during the meal a number of schoolchildren entertained the company by singing some verses, which gave them much pleasure. They then left ten Rixdollars for the children and continued their journey; dried peaches were handed out [10].

On 2 September Br. Kohrhammer preached the first sermon in the camp, in the open air. After that the Governor had a large tent put up, under which he, the preachers and the officers took their places. The Hottentots sat around the tent. When the corps was later ordered into the town to defend it, Br. Kohrhammer was released by the government with many thanks, and arrived back in Baviaanskloof on 12 October.

In a friendly letter the government asked us to take in the wives and children of the men in military service; the Governor's office would provide for their necessary upkeep. On the 11th the first transport from the camp at the Wynberg [11] arrived here. The sight of some [number missing] and 80 women, not counting the children, half starved and almost without clothing, moved us deeply, and we tried as far as we could to still their hunger at once and to find them shelter. On the 13th the second transport arrived and on the 15th the third, so that we now have 187 women and children, of all kinds of people, with us. This is no small burden for us. But in the hope that the seed of the Gospel might yet bear fruit in some of them, we gladly took on this difficulty and showed them mercy. The commander of the Hottentots, Le Sueur, sent us by express 20 Rixdollars from the soldiers in service for their wives and children, and it was distributed at once. A commission concerned with the distribution of grain also came to us; we distribute it weekly.

All the inhabitants of the country now had to take up arms for its defence, and to this end 24 Hottentot men were also called up here, leaving their wives and children behind. It grieves us to leave this poor people without care, but we must resign ourselves to it.

From the report on the Hottentot congregation, namely at Baviaanskloof, from December 1804 to May 1805.

Our Br. Rose's illness, which had lasted over a month until then, struck him down again at the beginning of December and caused a swelling [1], from which the patient suffered much pain.

Since a large part of our Hottentots had gone some time before to the neighbouring farmers to help with the harvest, we had to suspend the schools and some of the weekly meetings. The heat reached an extraordinary degree this month. At the same time there was such a drought that in several places no water was to be had for man or beast; it even came to the point that water had to be fetched from an hour away, from our own Hottentots [2], before a drop could be had.

On 11 December a transport of 28 newly recruited Hottentots passed through here. The commander of this very brave corps (the Hottentot corps), a [3] ... for some food for his hungry men, who were like their (?) [4], which we gave them as well as we could.

In these days a European sister, passing by chance, was called into a Hottentot hut and found there a Hottentot woman, lately arrived, lying ill. She said: "Here I lie, receiving from God the punishment I have deserved. I was told often enough what is right and what is wrong, but I would not listen; I stopped my heart and my ears. But now terror and trembling come over me when I think of dying, for I can expect nothing else than to be lost for ever." The sister urged her to thank God for revealing to her the state of her soul and for leaving her enough time to care for her eternal welfare. Now she should turn to her Redeemer without delay, for he would hear her, since his grace is greater than her sins and misery. The sick woman was very grateful for this encouragement and was visited often afterwards.

Another time one of us came to a widow lying at the point of death, and since she too admitted that she was afraid of death, he urged her in this situation to seek [the salvation of] her soul. That same evening, late, the sick woman sent for the brother and said to him: "I have thought about your words, and I must confess that I have heard the Word of God for many years but stayed indifferent to it. For that I now reproach myself bitterly; yes, and I am afraid to appear before God." She was encouraged to seek forgiveness through the blood of the Saviour, which offers mercy to her too. She then gave the brother her hand to show her gratitude. After a few days her illness changed so that she was no longer conscious, and before daybreak she died.

On Christmas Day many Hottentots from elsewhere and black slaves were present at the sermon. Some of our Christian neighbours were also with us for this festival. This came about because the preacher of Swellendam, whose church had been badly damaged when a powder magazine blew up and has not yet been fully repaired, was travelling round the country holding services in various places, including near us, and on that occasion many had their children baptised. All the more of our Christian fellow inhabitants came to us for the end of the year, among them several of our nearest neighbours who had never been here before. But large as the crowd was that gathered here, there was still much quiet and good order in the place and in the church.

In the year 1804, 14 adults and 15 children were baptised. 9 baptised persons were admitted to Holy Communion. Of the baptised, 13 persons went home [5]. At the end of the year 1804 our Hottentot congregation consisted of 245 baptised adults, of whom 94 are communicants, 129 baptised children and 99 candidates for baptism, 473 persons in all [6]. Altogether, according to a count taken in December, there lived here in 217 houses 186 men, 306 women and 601 children, 1093 persons in all, not counting the European Brethren and Sisters.

## January 1805



On the Festival of the Saviour [1], 6 January 1805, there was a baptism of 7 adults and 3 children in the morning. A sick man had already been [baptised] in his home that morning [2]. In the evening, in a prayer, we commended to the Saviour all our congregations among the heathen, with the men and women who serve them, and called on him to further the work of his hands everywhere on earth.

On the 7th a widowed sister fell asleep after a long illness. Although she had much to bear [3], she was always found full of trust in the dear Saviour, on whom she had set her only hope in hard days too. By her upright life she had earned the love and respect of old and young in the congregation.

The next day a 7-year-old girl, who had always been very sickly, also departed this life, in the end of consumption. This child was especially loving and patient in her illness, and in the last days of her life looked forward with all her heart to going home. The day before, she had all her acquaintances and playmates called to her, took a touching leave of them, and said she would soon go to her Saviour and find there all her friends who had gone before her. "I am heartily glad of it," she added; "here below I need nothing more than Christ's blood and righteousness."

Br. Rose's illness, which had reached a high degree about this time, broke towards the end of this month, and the patient grew better from day to day. From the middle of January the schools and meetings could again be held in their usual order.

On 19 January we received 4 wagonloads of grain from the government, which made us very glad, since it enabled us to give out to the wives and children of the Hottentots serving as soldiers the quantity set for each of them. Because the grain did not ripen at all in these parts last year on account of the great drought, and there is therefore great scarcity at present, the government has set up a special commission to see that the necessary grain is brought here. According to the proclamation issued on this, 1 pound of grain a day is reckoned per head; and since this is less than was originally set for the Hottentot women and children here, the government intends, as Governor Janssens soon informed us, to give one sheep a day for every 50 women. The only great difficulty at present is that, because the drought still continues, the livestock here find no grazing.

## February to Easter 1805



From time to time transports of newly engaged Hottentots passed through here. On 18 February a deserter was caught not far from our house. Another, who had run away with him, fled into the mountains. As soon as the news reached the Hottentot captain, he set out at once, as his duty required, with a number of armed Hottentots and pursued the fugitive all through the night, but could not lay hands on him, until he was caught and the next day taken by 5 men to Field Cornet Grebe [1].

About this time our gardeners had already been busy picking the peaches, and although we did not get as many this year as usual, we still have reason to thank God for his blessing, for in many neighbouring gardens the fruit was spoiled by worms or scorched by the sun and lost.

In these days an unbaptised sick Hottentot woman sent for a couple of the Brethren and Sisters in the night. Asked what she wanted, the sick woman said: "I shall go to the dear Saviour tonight. I am not afraid to appear before him [2], for he has forgiven me all my many sins; so I wish to be reconciled with the Brethren and Sisters." She was urged to stay on this ground of faith and hold fast to the Saviour. Early in the morning she sent word to them that she still felt the same peace in her heart and longed to be united with the Saviour. Soon afterwards, having taken a moving farewell of those present, she gave up her spirit; it was not long before she died.

In the middle of February we received letters from Germany and Denmark, several of them more than a year old, and in March the Daily Texts for 1805 came into our hands, though we could not find out how they reached us. The packet containing them was handed over by a farmer and was addressed to Messrs Schmidt and Kaeuser in Cape Town. There was no letter with it, but from the address we recognised the hand of Br. Latterup [3] in Copenhagen.

To the heartfelt joy of us all, Br. Rose had recovered from his illness so far that on 17 March he could go out for the first time, and on the 23rd could hold Holy Communion in the [4] meeting.

On the 19th a sister fell asleep who was loved by all for her childlike fellowship with the Saviour and her kindness towards everyone. On her sickbed she was wholly resigned to the will of her Redeemer, and what she said edified all who visited her.

We had few visitors from outside during this time, mainly, it seems, because the fields and meadows around us are so burnt by the long drought that the cattle find no more food, and travellers would therefore be in great difficulty.

Towards Easter, at Holy Communion, which we held on Maundy Thursday [5], many of our brothers and sisters expressed warmly and sincerely their heartfelt gratitude to the Saviour for giving our Br. Rose back to us, together with what they told us about their state of grace [6]. At the Easter festival a great crowd of people came to us and attended the meetings attentively. On the second day of the feast we had a baptism of 5 adults.

About this time we had a pleasant visit of eight days from the Dutch missionary Erasmus Schmidt, who was on his way, as appointed, to Dr van der Kemp at Bethelsdorp. He had left Holland back in 1802 with four other missionaries bound for Africa, but their ship was captured by the English and taken to America.

## April and May 1805



On 16 April we received a letter from Governor Janssens, asking us to send one of our number as preacher and teacher to the camp of the Hottentots at the Wynberg. We had weighed the difficulties of this matter carefully in these warlike times, and with Br. Rose still weak we could spare no one from among us; even so, it did not seem wise to refuse the request outright. Br. Rose therefore told the Governor in his answer only that we were at present unable to make the journey with our own draught oxen, since in the great drought our oxen had died for lack of fodder and the rest had lost all their strength. We would therefore await his further decisions.

In these days a number of the Hottentots stationed in the camp were here in Baviaanskloof visiting their wives and children.

On 29 May [1] a sister fell asleep, to whom all who knew her bore witness that she had stayed true to the grace she received in baptism. When a European sister visiting her asked whether she had anything on her heart concerning her coming departure, she answered: "I am ready to see my Saviour, but one worry makes dying hard for me: my husband is still so far from the Saviour. Help me pray that the Saviour will open his eyes." When she was advised to let this grief go and keep to herself alone, she grew quiet, and soon afterwards she died.

At the beginning of May the weather turned cold, and as a result many of our Hottentots fell ill. Br. Rose, too, grew worse again, and was soon so ill that we had to call in Dr Temple again in haste, who had faithfully attended him in his previous illness.

On 5 May an old, frail brother named Simeon [2] fell asleep, whom we have already mentioned in our reports. He had to be [brought] to the meetings on a wagon ... [3] venting his annoyance, which he did not easily get over, until his last illness obliged him to. In that illness we visited him often, and he could not thank us enough. In the last hours of his life he spoke with particular liveliness of his hope of seeing the Saviour soon, and cried out again and again: "Oh come, Lord Jesus!" He thanked his wife warmly for the faithful care he had had from her, and said: "Now it is coming to the end. Do not fret, for now my Saviour is coming soon to take my soul to himself." Soon afterwards he died.

About this time the missionary Christian Ulbricht stayed with us on a visit of some days [4]. He brought us greetings from our Brethren and Sisters in Zeist, and we found him a true lover of Jesus and a man faithfully devoted to his cause. When he left we gave him a Hottentot to go part of the way with him. By this man he wrote to us that he would never forget his visit here: he had learned much here that he hoped, by the Saviour's grace, to put into practice in future.

On 9 May Brother Kohrhammer [5] received a letter addressed to him personally from the War Commissioner, asking him in the friendliest way to go again with his wife to the camp at the Wynberg; Sub-lieutenant Grebe had already been instructed to arrange transport for them and their belongings at the government's expense. Soon afterwards, on the 16th, Field Cornet Grebe arrived here. He had already taken great trouble to find a wagon for Brother and Sister Kohrhammer, but in the present ploughing season could not get one. At last one of our Hottentots, named Matthäus, who was just about to take a load of wood to the Cape, was willing to unload the wood and take Brother and Sister Kohrhammer for 40 Rixdollars as far as Baviaanskloof [6]. So they left at 2 o'clock, after a heartfelt farewell, commended to God's grace and protection. On the 27th we heard from some Hottentots arriving here from the camp that the Brother and Sister had arrived there safely on the 25th.

In these days we are busy, following the Landdrost's instructions and to the satisfaction of all the inhabitants here, with taking possession of [our land] without much fuss, and with protecting its boundaries this year from new encroachments by the neighbours through sworn witnesses [7]. These returns are to be made every year on a set day, when the boundaries are walked anew. Until now we had only drawn up a list of persons. But since this is no longer enough, and 10 thalers have already been demanded as a contribution to the colony's treasury, we fear that we, and our poor Hottentots as well, who live in the bitterest poverty as it is, will be burdened with taxes.

On the 24th a government messenger, passing through on his way to the Cape, brought us the welcome news that the disputes that had arisen between the farmers and the Kaffers [8] had been settled amicably. Soon afterwards we heard that a commando of Hottentots, including some from here [9], had passed by at some distance on its way to the lower country, to guard the frontier there.

On the 27th we received a letter from Dr van der Kemp, who has been called to the Cape by the government. He promises to visit us as soon as he comes back, and to talk with us about this matter then.

In this month several children of our Hottentot brothers and sisters were brought by the Saviour to their blessed end. Among them was a boy of 10 whose mother, since he was unwell, had given him a purgative, an emetic commonly used here. The dose was presumably too strong, for the child was violently affected by it and died a few hours later.

## The death and burial of Brother Rose



Account of the death and burial of the late Brother Rose.

It was on Saturday, 12 October [1], at midday, that it pleased our dear Lord to call home from our midst his faithful servant, our dear Brother Christian Ludwig Rose, after a severe and lengthy illness. We accompanied his departing soul with the singing of some verses and gave him the blessing of the Lord, with a sense that our Saviour was near [2]. Then we thanked the Saviour in a prayer for all that he had accomplished here through this faithful servant, and called on him to comfort the widow left behind and soon to fill the late brother's place again.

After we had sent a messenger with this painful news to Brother and Sister Kohrhammer, the event was announced to the congregation, and the assembled Hottentots were urged to give themselves anew to the Saviour on this occasion, so that their teachers, who had proclaimed to them the way to salvation, might one day find them all again with the Saviour. A powerful sense of grace filled this meeting, and many tears of love and thanks were shed.

The next day the body was dressed, and in the evening we European Brethren and Sisters held a liturgy beside it, with a heart-melting sense of the nearness of Jesus. Many of the Hottentots who stood outside listening said that they had felt as if they were in heaven. Then the door was opened, and one group of Hottentots after another was let in to see the body, and there were heartbreaking scenes.

Meanwhile the Hottentots, of their own accord, struck up several verses, and those standing outside sang along with those in the room. The sweet fragrance [3] that arose, and the silence with which they then went home, made a deep impression on every heart. Most of them came back by day to see the uncovered remains of their departed teacher once more, and many lovely things were said. "It has taken hold of me," some said; "may I not throw away the grace of what he said to me on that occasion." Others said: "He often asked me whether I did not want to give myself to the Saviour too, and I never gave him that joy." Others again said: "Now he is with the Saviour, singing thanks that he so often proclaimed his love to us poor people."

On the 16th, the day set for the burial, many of our Christian neighbours came, partly out of friendship and partly out of curiosity, to see how the Brethren bury their dead. We kept the body in a room of our house until an hour before the burial, and all the strangers came to see it there and spoke of their pleasure at how neatly it was dressed. It was then carried into the church and set down in the middle aisle. After a short address, those present were told how the Saviour had drawn the late brother's heart to himself, how he had given himself to the Saviour, and for what our dear Lord had then used him in his vineyard. This was heard with great silence and attention. Then 10 baptised Hottentots set the body down in front of the hall [4], and when everyone had taken their places, the procession went in all quietness, singing some verses, to God's Acre [5], where the body was lowered into the grave with the liturgy customary among us. The Christians present could not hide their astonishment that the Hottentots laid the body in the earth with more reverence and decorum than they had ever seen even among Christians, and they concluded that the Hottentots must love their teacher very much.

We too were glad that not the slightest disorder occurred, since our people had never seen such a burial before. But they told us that they had asked the Saviour to help them, so that they need not be ashamed before the Christians present. They also said they had not believed that we would think them worthy to carry the body of our late brother to the grave.

#### Notes

1. The year is not given in this account. 12 October fell on a Saturday in 1805, and the report before it runs to May 1805, so the death was probably on 12 October 1805. To be confirmed from other records.
2. The transcription reads *unter dem nahen Erlebnis unsers Heilandes*, "under the near experience of our Saviour"; rendered by sense.
3. *Wohlgeruch*, "sweet fragrance", as transcribed. *Wohlklang*, "harmony" or "sweet sound", would fit the singing better and may be the true reading. To be checked against the image.
4. *vor dem Saal*: the church hall. The order of events is not fully clear: the body was already in the church for the address.
5. *Gottesacker*, "God's Acre": the Moravian name for a burial ground.
